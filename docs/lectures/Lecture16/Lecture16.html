Decision Trees for Healthcare Prediction

Published

January 1, 2026

1 Introduction: A Different Way to Think About Prediction

1.1 A Story: The Hospital Administrator’s Dilemma

Imagine you’re the CFO of a large hospital system. Every year, you need to budget for patient care costs. Some patients cost $2,000 annually, others cost $200,000. Your board wants accurate predictions so you can:

  • Set appropriate insurance premiums
  • Allocate resources to high-risk patients
  • Plan staffing and capacity

You have data on thousands of patients (e.g., their age, medical conditions, past emergency room visits, insurance type, etc). In prior lectures, we learned how to use (penalized) regression models. But here’s the problem: healthcare costs don’t work in simple, linear, additive ways.

Consider two patients:

  • Patient A: 25 years old with diabetes → might cost $3,000/year
  • Patient B: 75 years old with diabetes → might cost $30,000/year

The effect of diabetes is completely different depending on age! With regular regression, you’d have to manually tell the computer “create an age × diabetes interaction term.” But what if there are dozens of such interactions? You’d never find them all.

This is where decision trees shine: they automatically discover these complex patterns.

1.2 What Is a Decision Tree?

Note📘 Terminology: Decision Trees vs. Regression Trees

“Decision Tree” is the umbrella term for tree-based models. There are two main types:

  • Classification Trees: Predict categories (e.g., “Will this patient be readmitted?” → Yes/No)
  • Regression Trees: Predict continuous numbers (e.g., “What will this patient cost?” → $15,247)

In this lecture, we’ll cover regression trees because we’re predicting annual healthcare costs (a continuous dollar amount). However, the core concepts and methods apply to both types, so we’ll use “decision tree” as the general term throughout. Just remember: when we say “decision tree,” we specifically mean the regression tree variety!

Think of a decision tree like a game of “20 Questions” for predicting patient costs:

How to read this diagram:

  • Start at the top box (“Patient over 65?”)
  • Follow the arrows based on YES vs. NO answers
  • End at a blue prediction box (called a “leaf”)
  • Each question splits patients into smaller, more similar groups

Each final prediction is based on the average cost of similar patients in your historical data.

1.3 Why Decision Trees for Healthcare?

Decision trees are particularly powerful for healthcare analytics because:

1.3.1 1. They Think Like Clinicians

Doctors already use tree-like decision rules: “If patient has chest pain AND is over 50 AND has diabetes → run cardiac workup.” Trees formalize this intuitive reasoning.

1.3.2 2. They Handle Complexity Automatically

  • Interactions: Automatically discover whether a factor matters more depending on other factors (e.g., heart disease matters more for elderly patients)
  • Non-linear relationships: Don’t assume age has a constant effect across all ranges
  • Mixed data types: Handle both numbers (age, lab values) and categories (gender, insurance type)

1.3.3 3. They’re Easy to Explain

You can literally draw the tree and show it to decision makers. For example: “This patient is over 65, has been to the ER 3 times, and has heart disease, so we predict they will be high-cost.”

1.3.4 4. They Don’t Need Much Preparation

  • No need to standardize variables
  • Can handle missing data
  • No assumptions about data distributions

2 How Decision Trees Work

2.1 The Basic Idea: Divide and Conquer

Let’s build intuition with a simple example. Imagine you have 100 patients and you want to predict their annual healthcare costs. Here’s what a decision tree does:

Step 1: Start with everyone in one group

  • All 100 patients together
  • Average cost: $15,000
  • But there’s huge variation: some cost $1,000, others $100,000
  • We can measure how much error we have in predictions when only the average cost is used

Step 2: Find the best split

The algorithm looks at every variable and asks: “What single split would best separate high-cost from low-cost patients?”

Maybe it finds:

  • Patients with prior hospitalizations (n=20) → average $45,000
  • Patients without prior hospitalizations (n=80) → average $8,000

If these predictions are more accurate than predicting $15,000 for everyone, we use this split.

Step 3: Repeat for each group

Now take those 80 patients without prior hospitalizations. Can we do better than predicting $8,000 for all of them?

Maybe split by age:

  • Under 50 (n=50) → average $4,000
  • Over 50 (n=30) → average $14,000

Step 4: Keep going until groups are homogeneous

Continue this process until each final group (called a “leaf”) contains patients with similar healthcare costs.

2.2 How Does the Tree Decide Where to Split?

Simple Answer: It tries every possible split and picks the one that reduces prediction errors the most.

2.2.1 Example: Splitting on Age

Let’s say we’re considering: “Should we split at age 50?”

Before split: 100 patients, average cost = $15,000

  • Some patients cost $50,000, some cost $2,000
  • Our predictions are pretty bad!

After splitting at age 50:

  • Group 1 (Age < 50): 60 patients, average = $7,000
    • Most patients are close to $7,000
    • Prediction errors are smaller
  • Group 2 (Age ≥ 50): 40 patients, average = $26,000
    • Most patients are close to $26,000
    • Prediction errors are smaller

The algorithm measures whether our prediction errors get smaller. If yes, it’s a good split!

The algorithm tries EVERY possible split (age 30, age 31, age 32, etc., and also diabetes yes/no, prior ER visits 0 vs. 1+, etc.) and picks the winner.

2.3 Why “Recursive”?

Recursive means the algorithm repeats the same process over and over:

  1. Look at a group of patients
  2. Find the best way to split them into two groups
  3. For each new group, go back to step 1
  4. Stop when groups are small or homogeneous enough

Think of it like organizing a filing cabinet:

  • First drawer: “Active” vs. “Inactive” patients
  • Within “Active”: “High-risk” vs. “Low-risk”
  • Within “High-risk”: “Diabetes” vs. “No diabetes”
  • And so on…

Each level of the tree makes the groups more specific and similar.

2.4 Optimal Tree Size: Not Too Big, Not Too Small

2.4.1 The Danger of Trees That Are Too Big (Overfitting)

Imagine a tree that keeps splitting until each final group has only 1-2 patients:

Patient A, age 67, diabetic, 2 ER visits → Predicted cost: $43,127

  • This is patient A’s actual cost from last year!
  • But is this a good prediction for similar (new) patients?
  • No! Patient A might have had an unusual surgery that year or other conditions

This is overfitting: The tree has memorized the specific patients in your data, including all the random variation (lucky vs. unlucky years, unusual events, etc.). When we apply the same rules to new patients, this may not perform well.

2.4.2 The Danger of Trees That Are Too Small (Underfitting)

On the other hand, a tree with only 1-2 splits might be too simple:

All patients → Predicted cost: $15,000

  • This is useless! We know costs vary wildly
  • We’re ignoring useful information

This is underfitting: The tree is too simple to capture real patterns.

2.4.3 The Solution: Finding the “Just Right” or Optimal Tree Size

We need to find the sweet spot. Here’s how:

Step 1: Grow a large tree

  • Let it split many times
  • Don’t worry about overfitting yet

Step 2: Test different tree sizes

  • Prune back to different sizes (15 leaves, 10 leaves, 5 leaves, 3 leaves…)
  • For each size, measure prediction accuracy on separate test data

Step 3: Pick the size that performs best on test data

  • Not on training data (that would just give us the biggest tree)
  • On fresh data the tree hasn’t seen

Think of it like teaching someone to cook.

  • Too few instructions (“cook the chicken”) = underfit.
  • Too many instructions (“heat to exactly 347.2°F for 37 minutes and 12 seconds”) = overfit.
  • The right amount: “cook until internal temperature reaches 165°F and juices run clear” = just right!

2.4.4 Cross-Validation: The Smart Way to Find the Right Size

The Problem: Why We Can’t Use Test Data for Model Selection

You might think: “I’ll just try different tree sizes and pick the one that performs best on my test set!” But this creates a subtle problem:

Bad Approach:

  1. Try 5-leaf tree → test on test set → error = $8,000
  2. Try 10-leaf tree → test on test set → error = $7,500
  3. Try 15-leaf tree → test on test set → error = $7,200 ✓ Best!
  4. Pick 15-leaf tree and report: “Our model has $7,200 error”

You’ve now peeked at the test set multiple times and chose the model specifically because it performed well on this data. Your reported error ($7,200) is overly optimistic. It won’t reflect true performance on future patients you haven’t seen yet!

The Solution: Cross-Validation on Training Data Only

Keep your test set locked away until the very end! Instead:

  1. Split once: 70% training, 30% test (lock the test set away!)
  2. Within training data, split into 10 pieces for cross-validation (the number of folds can vary)
  3. For each tree size:
    • Train on 9 pieces, validate on the 10th
    • Repeat 10 times (each piece gets to be validation set)
    • Average the validation errors across all 10 folds
  4. Pick tree size with best average cross-validation error
  5. Build final model on ALL training data
  6. Finally, evaluate once on test set for honest performance

This way, the test set truly represents “future patients” (non-trained data) you haven’t peeked at!

2.5 When Should You Use Decision Trees?

Decision Trees Are GREAT For:

1. Explaining to Non-Technical Audiences

  • “Here’s exactly why we predict this patient is high-risk”
  • Can draw the tree easily (like in PowerPoint)
  • Creates clear, actionable patient segments

2. Complex, Interactive Relationships

  • When effects depend on context (diabetes matters more for elderly patients)
  • When you don’t know which interactions matter in advance
  • When relationships are non-linear (age 70→75 might matter more than 30→35)

3. Mixed Data Types

  • Combining numbers (age, lab values) and categories (gender, insurance)
  • No need to create dummy variables manually

4. Quick Prototyping

  • Fast to build and interpret
  • Good for initial exploration

Decision Trees Are LESS GREAT For:

1. When Relationships Are Smooth, Linear, and Additive

  • If cost really does just go up $250 per year of age for everyone (constant effect), linear regression would be simpler and more accurate

2. Small Datasets

  • Trees need enough data to split multiple times. In general, trees with <100 observations might not split well

3. When You Need Smooth Predictions

  • Trees create “step functions” where everyone in a group gets the same prediction. When smooth, continuous predictions are needed, tree models may not be ideal.

4. Extrapolation

  • Trees can’t predict beyond the range of your data. For example, if oldest patient in the data is 85, can’t reliably predict for 95-year-old.

3 Hands-On Tutorial: Building Decision Trees in R

3.1 Step 1: Loading Our Tools

Think of this like getting your tools ready before starting a project. We need special R packages, rpart (Recursive Partitioning And Regression Trees) for building and visualizing trees.

3.2 Step 2: Creating Our Patient Dataset

The Context: You’re analyzing a health system with 1,000 patients. You want to predict their annual healthcare costs based on things like age, medical conditions, and past utilization.

We’ll create simulated data that mimics real-world healthcare:

  • Complex relationships: Diabetes costs more for elderly patients than young ones (interaction!)
  • Realistic cost distribution: Most patients are low-cost, some are very expensive
  • Multiple types of information: Numbers (age), categories (insurance type), yes/no (diabetes)

Don’t worry about understanding every line of code here—focus on what we’re creating:

# Step 2a: Create 1,000 patients with various characteristics
n <- 1000  # Our sample size

healthcare_data <- data.frame(
  # Basic demographics
  age = round(runif(n, 18, 85)),  # Age between 18-85
  sex = sample(c("Male", "Female"), n, replace = TRUE),

  # Health indicators
  bmi = round(rnorm(n, 28, 6), 1),  # Body Mass Index
  smoker = sample(c(0, 1), n, replace = TRUE, prob = c(0.8, 0.2)),  # 20% smokers

  # Medical conditions (we'll fill these in next based on age)
  diabetes = NA,
  hypertension = NA,
  heart_disease = NA,

  # Insurance coverage
  insurance = sample(c("Private", "Medicare", "Medicaid", "Uninsured"),
                     n, replace = TRUE, prob = c(0.5, 0.25, 0.15, 0.1)),

  # Past healthcare use (higher numbers = more past utilization)
  prior_er_visits = rpois(n, lambda = 0.5),
  prior_hospitalizations = rpois(n, lambda = 0.15),

  # Geographic region
  region = sample(c("Northeast", "Midwest", "South", "West"), n, replace = TRUE)
)

# Step 2b: Generate medical conditions (more likely for older patients)
# This makes our data realistic - older people tend to have more conditions
healthcare_data <- healthcare_data %>%
  mutate(
    # Diabetes: more common with age and higher BMI
    diabetes = rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-3 + 0.03 * age + 0.05 * bmi)),

    # High blood pressure: more common with age
    hypertension = rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-2 + 0.04 * age)),

    # Heart disease: more common with age, smoking, and other conditions
    heart_disease = rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-4 + 0.05 * age + 0.5 * smoker +
                                         0.3 * diabetes + 0.3 * hypertension)),

    # Total number of chronic conditions per patient
    n_conditions = diabetes + hypertension + heart_disease
  )

# Step 2c: Generate annual healthcare costs
# We're building in complex patterns that our decision tree will discover:
# - Non-linear age effects (costs accelerate with age)
# - Interactions (conditions cost more for elderly patients)
# - Variation (some randomness, like in real life)

healthcare_data <- healthcare_data %>%
  mutate(
    # Base cost increases with age (and accelerates - that's the age^2 part)
    base_cost = 1000 + 50 * age + 0.5 * age^2,

    # Costs from chronic conditions
    # IMPORTANT: Notice we multiply age × n_conditions - this is an INTERACTION
    # Conditions cost more for older patients!
    condition_cost = 3000 * diabetes + 2500 * hypertension +
                     5000 * heart_disease +
                     50 * age * n_conditions,  # <-- The interaction

    # Costs from past healthcare use
    utilization_cost = 2000 * prior_er_visits + 15000 * prior_hospitalizations,

    # Costs from behaviors
    behavior_cost = 1500 * smoker + 50 * pmax(bmi - 30, 0),

    # Insurance affects recorded costs
    insurance_effect = case_when(
      insurance == "Private" ~ 1.0,
      insurance == "Medicare" ~ 1.1,  # Medicare patients use more services
      insurance == "Medicaid" ~ 0.9,
      insurance == "Uninsured" ~ 0.6  # Uninsured use less (unfortunately)
    ),

    # Combine everything to get final cost
    expected_cost = (base_cost + condition_cost + utilization_cost +
                     behavior_cost) * insurance_effect,

    # Add some randomness (real life isn't perfectly predictable!)
    annual_cost = round(expected_cost * exp(rnorm(n, 0, 0.3)))
  ) %>%
  # Remove intermediate calculation columns (we just need annual_cost)
  select(-base_cost, -condition_cost, -utilization_cost,
         -behavior_cost, -insurance_effect, -expected_cost)

# Step 2d: Convert text variables to proper format for R
# R needs categorical variables (like "Male"/"Female") as "factors"
healthcare_data <- healthcare_data %>%
  mutate(
    sex = factor(sex),
    insurance = factor(insurance),
    region = factor(region),
    smoker = factor(smoker, labels = c("No", "Yes")),
    diabetes = factor(diabetes, labels = c("No", "Yes")),
    hypertension = factor(hypertension, labels = c("No", "Yes")),
    heart_disease = factor(heart_disease, labels = c("No", "Yes"))
  )

3.3 Step 4: Building Your First Decision Tree

This is the exciting part! We’re going to build a decision tree that automatically discovers patterns in our data.

3.3.1 The Basic Command

Building a tree in R is surprisingly simple. We can use the rpart function in R. It’s similar to the lm() function you learned for regression:

# Build the decision tree!
# This is the main command - notice it looks like lm()
cost_tree <- rpart(
  # Formula: predict annual_cost using all these variables
  formula = annual_cost ~ age + sex + bmi + smoker + diabetes +
            hypertension + heart_disease + insurance +
            prior_er_visits + prior_hospitalizations + region,

  # Which data to use
  data = healthcare_data,

  # method = "anova" means we're predicting a number (not a category)
  method = "anova"

  # Note: Cross-validation happens automatically!
  # rpart performs 10-fold CV by default (controlled by xval = 10 parameter)
  # The CV results are stored in cost_tree$cptable (we'll see this later)
)

# Look at the text summary (not very readable, we'll visualize it next!)
print(cost_tree)

Trees are meant to be seen, not read as text. Let’s create a visual diagram using the rpart.plot function:

# Create a beautiful tree diagram!
rpart.plot(
  cost_tree,
  type = 4,                 # Show all the information
  extra = 101,              # Show number and percentage of patients in each group
  under = TRUE,             # Put numbers under the boxes (easier to read)
  fallen.leaves = TRUE,     # Line up the bottom boxes

  box.palette = "Blues",    # Color scheme (blue boxes)
  shadow.col = "gray",      # Add subtle shadows
  main = "Decision Tree for Healthcare Cost Prediction",
  cex = 0.8,                # Text size
  roundint = TRUE,          # Round to integers (no decimals for dollar amounts)
  digits = 5                # Use up to 5 digits (prevents scientific notation)
)

3.3.3 How to Read This Tree Diagram

Think of it like a flowchart for patient triage:

  1. Start at the top box (the “root”)
    • Contains ALL patients
    • Shows average cost for everyone
  2. Follow the labeled arrows
    • Each split asks a yes/no question
    • Follow the arrow labeled YES or NO based on the patient’s characteristics
    • The arrows show you which path to take
  3. End at a bottom box (a “leaf”)
    • This is your prediction!
    • Shows the average cost for patients in that group
  4. The deeper you go, the more specific the groups
    • First split: broad groups (e.g., old vs. young)
    • Later splits: narrow groups (e.g., elderly diabetics who’ve been hospitalized)

3.4 Controlling Tree Complexity

3.4.1 Understanding the Control Parameters

The rpart() function has several parameters that control how the tree grows. Understanding these is crucial for building effective models:

# Display default control parameters
defaults <- rpart.control()

Default rpart Control Parameters:

The rpart function has several “knobs” you can turn to control tree growth:

1. minsplit (default = 20)

  • Minimum observations required to attempt a split (i.e., “Don’t split groups smaller than 20 patients”)
  • Lower values → more splits → larger trees

2. minbucket (default = 7)

  • Minimum observations in any terminal node (leaf; i.e., “Each final group must have at least 7 patients”)
  • Lower values → smaller final groups → more overfitting risk

3. cp (complexity parameter, default = 0.01)

  • Controls pruning - this is the most important parameter!
  • Lower values → less pruning → larger trees
  • We’ll use cross-validation to find the optimal cp

4. maxdepth (default = 30)

  • Maximum depth of tree (number of levels)
  • Higher values → deeper trees → more interactions captured
  • Usually not a limiting factor

3.4.2 The Complexity Parameter (cp)

The complexity parameter cp is the most important tuning parameter. It controls pruning - how the tree decides whether a split is worth keeping:

  • A split is only kept if it reduces prediction errors by a meaningful amount (controlled by cp)
  • Higher cp → fewer splits → simpler trees
  • Lower cp → more splits → complex trees

Let’s see how cp affects tree size:

# Fit trees with different cp values
par(mfrow = c(2, 2))

cp_values <- c(0.1, 0.03, 0.01, 0.001)

for (cp_val in cp_values) {
  tree_temp <- rpart(
    annual_cost ~ age + sex + bmi + smoker + diabetes +
                  hypertension + heart_disease + insurance +
                  prior_er_visits + prior_hospitalizations + region,
    data = healthcare_data,
    method = "anova",
    control = rpart.control(cp = cp_val)
  )

  # Count terminal nodes
  n_leaves <- sum(tree_temp$frame$var == "<leaf>")

  rpart.plot(tree_temp,
             main = paste0("cp = ", cp_val, " (", n_leaves, " leaves)"),
             cex = 0.6,
             roundint = TRUE,
             digits = 5)
}
par(mfrow = c(1, 1))

As cp decreases, the tree grows larger with more terminal nodes. The challenge is finding the right balance—enough complexity to capture true patterns but not so much that we overfit.

3.4.3 Cross-Validation for Optimal Pruning

Tip🔍 Where Does Cross-Validation Happen?

Answer: Automatically inside rpart()!

When you call rpart(), it performs 10-fold cross-validation behind the scenes and stores the results in the tree’s $cptable component. You don’t need to explicitly code the CV - it happens automatically!

If you want to control it: - xval = 10 (default): 10-fold cross-validation - xval = 5: 5-fold cross-validation - xval = 0: Turn off CV (not recommended!)

Understanding the Cross-Validation Table (cptable):

Each row shows results for a different tree size:

  • CP: Complexity parameter value
  • nsplit: Number of splits in the tree
  • rel error: Training error (relative to simplest tree)
  • xerror: Cross-validated error (what we care about!)
  • xstd: Standard error of the CV error

What to look for: The tree size (nsplit) that has the lowest xerror!

3.4.4 Visualizing Cross-Validation Results

Let’s plot the cross-validation errors to find the optimal tree size:

# Plot cross-validation results
plotcp(full_tree)
# Find the cp with lowest cross-validation error
cp_table <- full_tree$cptable
optimal_cp <- cp_table[which.min(cp_table[, "xerror"]), "CP"]

How to Read This Plot:

  • X-axis (top): Size of tree (number of terminal nodes)
  • X-axis (bottom): Corresponding cp values
  • Y-axis: Cross-validation error (relative scale)
  • Dotted horizontal line: Minimum CV error + 1 standard error (1-SE rule threshold)
  • Goal: Find the lowest point on the curve (minimum xerror)

What we’re doing: We pick the cp that corresponds to the lowest point on this curve. That’s our optimal tree complexity!

Note: The dotted line shows the 1-SE rule threshold (a more conservative approach), but we’re using the simpler method of just picking the minimum point, which is 0.0024461.

# Prune to optimal cp

3.5 Evaluating Model Performance

3.5.1 Train-Test Split Approach

For a fair evaluation, we should test our model on data it hasn’t seen during training. Let’s split our data and evaluate performance properly. Same as others, let’s use a 70:30 split.

# Create train-test split (70-30)
train_idx <- sample(1:nrow(healthcare_data), 0.7 * nrow(healthcare_data))
train_data <- healthcare_data[train_idx, ]
test_data <- healthcare_data[-train_idx, ]

Data Split:

  • Training set: 700 patients (70%) - used to build the tree
  • Test set: 300 patients (30%) - held out to evaluate performance

This split ensures we evaluate the model on patients it has never seen before!

3.5.2 Building and Evaluating the Tree

Let’s build a tree model using the training dataset.

# Fit tree on training data
tree_train <- rpart(
  annual_cost ~ age + sex + bmi + smoker + diabetes +
                hypertension + heart_disease + insurance +
                prior_er_visits + prior_hospitalizations + region,
  data = train_data,
  method = "anova",
  control = rpart.control(cp = 0.001)
)

# Find optimal cp (lowest cross-validation error)
best_cp <- tree_train$cptable[which.min(tree_train$cptable[,"xerror"]), "CP"]

# Prune the tree to optimal size
tree_final <- prune(tree_train, cp = best_cp)

# Make predictions on test set
predictions <- predict(tree_final, test_data)

# Calculate performance metrics
actual <- test_data$annual_cost

# Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) - our primary metric
rmse <- sqrt(mean((actual - predictions)^2))

# R-squared - how much variation we explain
ss_res <- sum((actual - predictions)^2)
ss_tot <- sum((actual - mean(actual))^2)
r_squared <- 1 - (ss_res / ss_tot)

Test Set Performance:

How well does our tree predict costs for new patients? We’ll focus on two key metrics:

1. RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error): $8,498

  • This is the most common metric for comparing prediction models
  • Interpretation: “On average, our predictions are off by about $8,498”
  • Lower is better!

2. R-squared: 0.601

  • You’ve seen this before in regression!
  • Interpretation: “Our model explains 60.06% of the variation in healthcare costs”
  • Higher is better (ranges from 0 to 1)

4 Summary and Key Takeaways

4.1 What We Learned Today

1. Decision Trees Are a Different Way to Think About Prediction

  • Instead of equations, we create groups of similar patients
  • Each group gets a prediction based on historical averages
  • Works like a game of “20 questions” to classify patients

2. Trees Automatically Find Complex Patterns

  • They discover interactions (diabetes costs more for elderly patients)
  • They handle non-linear relationships (age effects that accelerate)
  • No need to manually specify these patterns!

3. Trees Are Easy to Explain

  • Visual diagrams anyone can understand
  • Clear decision rules for each prediction
  • Great for presenting to non-technical stakeholders

4. But Trees Need Careful Tuning

  • Too big → overfitting (memorizing noise)
  • Too small → underfitting (missing patterns)
  • Use cross-validation to find an optimal size

5. Trees vs. Regression: Different Tools for Different Jobs

  • Trees: Complex interactions, automatic feature discovery, easy to explain
  • Regression: Smooth relationships, smaller datasets, interpretable coefficients
  • Try both and compare!
