Bivariate Relationships: Crosstabulation and Correlation

MBA642: Business Analytics in Healthcare Management

Published

January 1, 2026

Introduction to Bivariate Relationships

Why Study Relationships Between Variables?

In healthcare management, understanding how variables relate to each other is fundamental to making evidence-based decisions. While descriptive statistics tell us about individual variables and t-tests or ANOVA compare group means, we often need to understand whether two variables are associated with each other. This understanding can reveal patterns that inform policy decisions, resource allocation, quality improvement initiatives, and strategic planning.

Consider these questions that healthcare managers frequently encounter:

  • Is there an association between a patient’s insurance type and whether they are readmitted within 30 days?
  • Do patients who wait longer in the emergency department report lower satisfaction scores?
  • Is there a relationship between nurse staffing levels and hospital-acquired infection rates?

These questions require us to examine bivariate relationships—the association between two variables measured on the same subjects or units.

Choosing the Right Method

The method we use to examine bivariate relationships depends on the measurement scale of our variables:

Variable Types Appropriate Method Example
Two nominal (categorical) variables Crosstabulation (Chi-square test) Insurance type × Readmission status
Two continuous/ordinal variables Correlation (Pearson or Spearman) Wait time × Satisfaction score

This document covers both methods, explaining when to use each, how to conduct the analysis in R, and how to interpret and report results in healthcare management contexts.

Part 1: Crosstabulation (Chi-Square Test)

What is Crosstabulation?

Crosstabulation (also called a contingency table or cross-tab) is a statistical method that tests whether there is a significant association between two categorical (nominal) variables. It answers the question: Are these two categorical variables independent of each other, or is there a systematic relationship between them?

The chi-square test of independence is the inferential statistical test used with crosstabulation. It compares the observed frequencies in each cell of the table to the expected frequencies that would occur if the two variables were completely independent.

The Logic Behind the Test

The chi-square test works by comparing what we actually observe in our data to what we would expect to see if there were no relationship between the variables:

  • If the variables are independent (no association): The distribution of one variable should look the same across all categories of the other variable
  • If the variables are associated (dependent): The distribution of one variable will differ across categories of the other variable

For example, if insurance type and readmission are independent, we would expect the readmission rate to be roughly the same regardless of insurance type. If they are associated, readmission rates would differ by insurance type.

When to Use Crosstabulation

Crosstabulation is appropriate when you want to test the association between two categorical variables. Common healthcare management applications include:

  • Quality outcomes: Is there an association between treatment protocol (Protocol A vs. Protocol B) and patient outcome (Improved vs. Not Improved)?
  • Service utilization: Is there an association between patient demographic group and choice of service delivery method (In-person vs. Telehealth)?
  • Operational decisions: Is there an association between shift type (Day vs. Night) and incident occurrence (Yes vs. No)?
  • Patient behavior: Is there an association between receiving a follow-up reminder (Yes vs. No) and appointment attendance (Attended vs. No-show)?

Key Assumption

For the chi-square test to be valid, each cell in the contingency table should have an expected frequency of at least 5. When this assumption is violated (often with small sample sizes), Fisher’s exact test should be used instead (discussed later in this document).

Scenario: Insurance Type and Hospital Readmission

A hospital quality improvement team wants to understand whether there is an association between patients’ insurance type (Medicare, Medicaid, Private) and 30-day readmission status (Readmitted vs. Not Readmitted). Understanding this relationship could help the hospital develop targeted interventions to reduce readmissions for high-risk groups.

The team collected data from 300 patients discharged from the cardiology unit over the past year.

The Chi-Square Formula

The chi-square statistic is calculated as:

\[\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}\]

Where:

  • \(O_{ij}\): Observed frequency in cell (i, j)
  • \(E_{ij}\): Expected frequency in cell (i, j)

The expected frequency for each cell is calculated as:

\[E_{ij} = \frac{(\text{Row Total}_i) \times (\text{Column Total}_j)}{\text{Grand Total}}\]

Understanding the Formula

The chi-square statistic measures the overall discrepancy between observed and expected frequencies:

  • Numerator \((O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2\): The squared difference between what we observed and what we expected. Squaring ensures all differences are positive and gives more weight to larger discrepancies.
  • Denominator \(E_{ij}\): Dividing by the expected frequency standardizes the difference, so cells with larger expected frequencies don’t dominate the statistic.

A larger chi-square value indicates a greater discrepancy between observed and expected frequencies, suggesting that the variables are likely associated. The p-value tells us the probability of observing such a large chi-square value (or larger) if the variables were truly independent.

Example: Insurance Type and Readmission

Let’s analyze our hospital readmission data:

# Create the dataset
set.seed(2026)

# Generate data where there IS an association
n <- 300

# Create insurance type
insurance <- sample(c("Medicare", "Medicaid", "Private"), n,
                    replace = TRUE, prob = c(0.4, 0.3, 0.3))

# Create readmission with different probabilities by insurance
readmission <- character(n)
for(i in 1:n) {
  if(insurance[i] == "Medicare") {
    readmission[i] <- sample(c("Readmitted", "Not Readmitted"), 1,
                              prob = c(0.25, 0.75))
  } else if(insurance[i] == "Medicaid") {
    readmission[i] <- sample(c("Readmitted", "Not Readmitted"), 1,
                              prob = c(0.30, 0.70))
  } else {
    readmission[i] <- sample(c("Readmitted", "Not Readmitted"), 1,
                              prob = c(0.12, 0.88))
  }
}

# Create data frame
readmission_data <- data.frame(
  insurance = factor(insurance, levels = c("Medicare", "Medicaid", "Private")),
  readmission = factor(readmission, levels = c("Readmitted", "Not Readmitted"))
)

Conducting the Chi-Square Test in R

We use the chisq.test() function to perform the chi-square test of independence:

# Perform chi-square test
chi_result <- chisq.test(table(readmission_data$insurance,
                                readmission_data$readmission))

# Display results
chi_result

Examining Expected Frequencies

It’s important to check that our expected frequencies meet the assumption (all cells ≥ 5):

# View expected frequencies
chi_result$expected
          
           Readmitted Not Readmitted
  Medicare       25.2          100.8
  Medicaid       16.4           65.6
  Private        18.4           73.6

All expected frequencies are greater than 5, so the chi-square test is appropriate for this data.

Interpreting the Results

Let’s break down the output:

  • X-squared (12.222): This is the chi-square test statistic, measuring the overall discrepancy between observed and expected frequencies.

  • df (2): Degrees of freedom, calculated as (rows - 1) × (columns - 1) = (3 - 1) × (2 - 1) = 2.

  • p-value (0.00222): The probability of observing a chi-square value this large or larger if the variables were truly independent.

Conclusion

Since the p-value (0.00222) is less than 0.05, we reject the null hypothesis of independence. There is a statistically significant association between insurance type and 30-day readmission status.

This finding suggests that the hospital should investigate why readmission rates differ by insurance type. Possible explanations might include differences in: - Access to follow-up care - Medication adherence support - Social determinants of health - Discharge planning resources

Fisher’s Exact Test vs. Chi-Square Test

When to Use Fisher’s Exact Test

The chi-square test relies on an approximation that works well with large samples. However, when expected frequencies are small (typically when any expected cell count is less than 5), this approximation becomes unreliable. In such cases, Fisher’s exact test should be used instead.

Fisher’s exact test calculates the exact probability of observing the data (or more extreme data) under the null hypothesis of independence, without relying on the chi-square approximation.

How Fisher’s Exact Test Works

Fisher’s exact test uses the hypergeometric distribution to calculate the exact probability of obtaining the observed arrangement of frequencies (or a more extreme arrangement) in a contingency table, given that the row and column totals are fixed.

For a 2×2 contingency table:

Outcome A Outcome B Row Total
Group 1 a b a + b
Group 2 c d c + d
Column Total a + c b + d n

The probability of observing exactly this arrangement is calculated using the hypergeometric formula:

\[P = \frac{(a+b)! \cdot (c+d)! \cdot (a+c)! \cdot (b+d)!}{n! \cdot a! \cdot b! \cdot c! \cdot d!}\]

Where: - The numerator represents the product of all row and column marginal factorials - The denominator represents the total sample factorial times each cell frequency factorial

The p-value is then calculated by summing the probabilities of all possible table arrangements that are equally extreme or more extreme than the observed table (i.e., arrangements that show the same or stronger association), while keeping the marginal totals fixed.

Understanding the Logic

The key insight is that Fisher’s exact test asks: “If the null hypothesis of independence were true (i.e., the row variable has no effect on the column variable), what is the probability of seeing data this extreme or more extreme, given our fixed marginal totals?”

Unlike the chi-square test which uses an approximation based on the chi-square distribution, Fisher’s test computes this probability exactly by enumerating all possible tables—making it reliable even with very small samples.

Consider a pilot study examining whether a new discharge protocol affects readmission:

                                   Readmission = c("Readmitted", "Not Readmitted")))

Interpreting Fisher’s Exact Test Output

  • p-value: The exact probability of observing this table (or more extreme) if the variables were independent. If p < 0.05, we reject independence and conclude there is a significant association.

  • Odds ratio: Fisher’s test also provides an odds ratio, which quantifies the strength of association. An odds ratio of 1 indicates no association; values far from 1 indicate stronger associations.

  • 95% confidence interval for odds ratio: If this interval does not contain 1, the association is statistically significant.

Key Differences

Aspect Chi-Square Test Fisher’s Exact Test
Sample size Large samples (expected frequencies ≥ 5) Any sample size, especially small
Calculation Uses chi-square approximation Calculates exact probabilities using hypergeometric distribution
Output Chi-square statistic, df, p-value Exact p-value, odds ratio, confidence interval
Table size Any size contingency table Originally 2×2, but R handles larger
When to use Default for large samples When expected frequencies < 5

Practical Guideline

Use this decision rule:

  1. First, run chisq.test() and examine the expected frequencies
  2. If all expected frequencies ≥ 5: Report the chi-square test results
  3. If any expected frequency < 5: Use fisher.test() instead

Part 2: Correlation Analysis

What is Correlation?

Correlation analysis tests whether there is a significant linear association between two continuous or ordinal variables. It quantifies both the direction (positive or negative) and strength of the relationship.

Unlike crosstabulation which examines categorical variables, correlation examines whether two numeric variables tend to change together in a consistent, linear pattern:

  • Positive correlation: As one variable increases, the other tends to increase
  • Negative correlation: As one variable increases, the other tends to decrease
  • No correlation: No consistent linear pattern between the variables

Important Distinction: Correlation vs. Causation

A critical concept to understand is that correlation does not imply causation. Just because two variables are correlated does not mean that one causes the other. There may be:

  • A third variable causing both (confounding)
  • Reverse causation
  • Coincidental association

For example, if we find that hospitals with more administrators have lower mortality rates, we cannot conclude that hiring more administrators reduces mortality. Both might be related to hospital size, funding, or other factors.

The Correlation Coefficient

The correlation coefficient is a standardized measure that ranges from -1 to +1:

\[r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \bar{X})(Y_i - \bar{Y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \bar{X})^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i - \bar{Y})^2}}\]

Or equivalently:

\[r = \frac{Cov(X, Y)}{SD(X) \times SD(Y)}\]

Interpreting the Correlation Coefficient

The correlation coefficient tells us three things:

  1. Direction (sign):
    • Positive (+): Variables move in the same direction
    • Negative (-): Variables move in opposite directions
  2. Strength (absolute value):
    • 0.00 - 0.19: Very weak or no correlation
    • 0.20 - 0.39: Weak correlation
    • 0.40 - 0.59: Moderate correlation
    • 0.60 - 0.79: Strong correlation
    • 0.80 - 1.00: Very strong correlation
  3. Statistical significance (p-value):
    • p < 0.05: The correlation is statistically significant
    • p ≥ 0.05: The correlation is not statistically significant

Note: These strength guidelines are general rules of thumb. Context matters—a correlation of 0.3 might be very meaningful in some healthcare contexts and trivial in others.

Pearson vs. Spearman Correlation

Pearson Correlation

Pearson correlation measures the linear relationship between two continuous (interval or ratio) variables. It uses the raw data values and assumes:

  • Both variables are normally distributed (or large sample size)
  • The relationship is linear
  • Homoscedasticity (constant variance)

Use Pearson correlation for variables like: - Patient age and length of stay - Wait time in minutes and satisfaction score - Staffing hours and number of patient falls

Spearman Correlation

Spearman correlation measures the monotonic relationship between two variables using ranks instead of raw values. It’s appropriate for:

  • Ordinal variables (e.g., pain scale ratings, satisfaction categories)
  • Continuous data with outliers (ranks minimize outlier influence)
  • Non-linear but monotonic relationships

Use Spearman correlation for variables like: - Education level (ordinal) and health literacy score - Pain rating (1-10 scale) and medication requests - When you suspect outliers might influence Pearson correlation

When to Use Correlation

Correlation is appropriate when examining the relationship between two non-categorical variables. Common healthcare management applications include:

  • Operational efficiency: Is there a relationship between nurse-to-patient ratio and patient satisfaction scores?
  • Resource utilization: Is there a relationship between length of stay and total charges?
  • Quality metrics: Is there a relationship between hand hygiene compliance rates and hospital-acquired infection rates?
  • Patient outcomes: Is there a relationship between wait time and patient satisfaction?

Scenario: Nurse Staffing and Patient Satisfaction

A hospital administrator wants to understand whether there is a relationship between nurse staffing levels (measured as nursing hours per patient day) and patient satisfaction scores (measured on a 0-100 scale). This analysis could inform staffing decisions and budget allocation.

Data was collected from 50 nursing units across a health system over the past quarter.

Example: Nurse Staffing and Satisfaction

# Generate realistic healthcare data
set.seed(2026)
n_units <- 50

# Nursing hours per patient day (typically 6-12 hours)
nursing_hours <- round(runif(n_units, 6, 12), 1)

# Patient satisfaction (correlated with nursing hours, with noise)
# Higher staffing generally associated with higher satisfaction
satisfaction <- round(50 + 4 * nursing_hours + rnorm(n_units, 0, 8), 1)
# Ensure scores are within 0-100 range
satisfaction <- pmin(pmax(satisfaction, 0), 100)

staffing_data <- data.frame(
  unit_id = 1:n_units,
  nursing_hours = nursing_hours,
  satisfaction = satisfaction
)

# View summary

Conducting Pearson Correlation in R

We use the cor.test() function to calculate the correlation coefficient and test its significance:

# Pearson correlation test
pearson_result <- cor.test(staffing_data$nursing_hours,
                           staffing_data$satisfaction,
                           method = "pearson")

Interpreting the Results

  • Correlation coefficient (r) = 0.677: This indicates a strong positive correlation between nursing hours and patient satisfaction.

  • t-statistic = 6.376: The test statistic used to determine significance.

  • p-value = 6.66e-08: The probability of observing this correlation if there were truly no relationship.

  • 95% Confidence Interval [0.491, 0.804]: We are 95% confident the true population correlation falls within this range.

Conclusion

Since the p-value is less than 0.05, we conclude that there is a statistically significant positive correlation between nursing hours per patient day and patient satisfaction scores. Units with higher staffing levels tend to have higher patient satisfaction.

This finding supports the business case for adequate nurse staffing—not only for patient safety but also for patient experience metrics that may affect hospital reimbursement and reputation.

Conducting Spearman Correlation in R

When working with ordinal data or when you suspect outliers, use Spearman correlation:

# Example: Pain level (ordinal 1-10) and mobility score (ordinal 1-5)

  mobility_score = mobility_score

Limitations of Correlation Analysis

1. Correlation is NOT Causation

This cannot be emphasized enough. A significant correlation between two variables does not mean one causes the other.

Healthcare Example: We might find that hospitals with more parking spaces have lower mortality rates. This doesn’t mean building parking garages saves lives—larger hospitals tend to have both more parking and more specialized services.

Always consider: - Confounding variables: Third variables that influence both - Reverse causation: Maybe the outcome influences the predictor - Selection bias: How the sample was selected might create artificial associations

2. Correlation Only Captures Linear Relationships

A correlation coefficient of zero means no linear relationship—there could still be a strong non-linear relationship.

# Example of non-linear relationship
workload <- seq(1, 10, length.out = 100)
# Performance increases then decreases (inverted U)
performance <- -0.5 * (workload - 5.5)^2 + 15 + rnorm(100, 0, 1)

nonlinear_data <- data.frame(workload = workload, performance = performance)

# Calculate correlation
nonlinear_cor <- cor(nonlinear_data$workload, nonlinear_data$performance)

ggplot(nonlinear_data, aes(x = workload, y = performance)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.6, color = "#2E86AB") +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", se = FALSE, color = "#E63946", linetype = "dashed") +
  geom_smooth(method = "loess", se = FALSE, color = "#2A9D8F") +
  labs(title = "Example: Non-Linear Relationship with Near-Zero Correlation",
       subtitle = paste("Pearson r =", round(nonlinear_cor, 3),
                       "- but there's clearly a relationship!"),
       x = "Workload Level",
       y = "Performance Score") +
  theme_minimal() +
  theme(plot.title = element_text(face = "bold"))

The inverted U-shape (common in stress-performance relationships) shows a clear pattern, but the linear correlation is near zero.

3. Pearson Correlation is Sensitive to Outliers

Extreme values can dramatically influence Pearson correlation coefficients—sometimes creating the appearance of a significant correlation where none truly exists. This is particularly dangerous because it can lead to incorrect conclusions.

Example: Outlier Creates Spurious Correlation

Consider examining the relationship between hospital size (number of beds) and patient satisfaction scores. Without an outlier, there may be no relationship, but a single extreme observation can create a statistically significant—but misleading—correlation:

# Data with NO real relationship (random scatter)
set.seed(42)
n_hospitals <- 15

beds_clean <- c(50, 75, 100, 120, 150, 180, 200, 220, 250, 280, 300, 350, 400, 450, 500)
satisfaction_clean <- c(78, 82, 75, 80, 77, 83, 79, 76, 81, 78, 80, 77, 82, 79, 81)

# Add one outlier that creates a spurious positive correlation
beds_with_outlier <- c(beds_clean, 600)
satisfaction_with_outlier <- c(satisfaction_clean, 95)  # Outlier: large hospital with very high satisfaction

# Calculate correlations and significance
cor_clean <- cor.test(beds_clean, satisfaction_clean)
cor_with_outlier <- cor.test(beds_with_outlier, satisfaction_with_outlier)
cor_spearman <- cor.test(beds_with_outlier, satisfaction_with_outlier, method = "spearman")

par(mfrow = c(1, 2))

# Plot without outlier - no significant correlation
plot(beds_clean, satisfaction_clean, pch = 19, col = "#2E86AB",
     main = paste0("Without Outlier\nr = ", round(cor_clean$estimate, 3),
                  ", p = ", round(cor_clean$p.value, 3)),
     xlab = "Number of Beds", ylab = "Satisfaction Score",
     xlim = c(0, 650), ylim = c(70, 100))
abline(lm(satisfaction_clean ~ beds_clean), col = "#E63946", lwd = 2)

# Plot with outlier - spurious significant correlation
plot(beds_with_outlier, satisfaction_with_outlier, pch = 19, col = "#2E86AB",
     main = paste0("With Outlier\nPearson r = ", round(cor_with_outlier$estimate, 3),
                  ", p = ", round(cor_with_outlier$p.value, 3)),
     xlab = "Number of Beds", ylab = "Satisfaction Score",
     xlim = c(0, 650), ylim = c(70, 100))
points(600, 95, pch = 19, col = "#E63946", cex = 2)  # Highlight the outlier
abline(lm(satisfaction_with_outlier ~ beds_with_outlier), col = "#E63946", lwd = 2)
text(600, 92, "Outlier", col = "#E63946", pos = 1)
par(mfrow = c(1, 1))
Comparison of Results:
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Without outlier:  Pearson r = 0.208, p = 0.457 (Not significant)
With outlier:     Pearson r = 0.570, p = 0.021 (Significant!)
With outlier:     Spearman r = 0.318, p = 0.231 (Not significant)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────

What happened here?

  • Without the outlier: The Pearson correlation is very weak and not statistically significant (p > 0.05). There is no evidence of a relationship between hospital size and satisfaction.

  • With the outlier: A single extreme point (a large hospital with unusually high satisfaction) creates a statistically significant correlation (p < 0.05). This would lead us to incorrectly conclude that larger hospitals have higher satisfaction scores.

  • Spearman correlation: Because Spearman uses ranks instead of raw values, the outlier has much less influence. The Spearman correlation correctly shows no significant relationship.

This example demonstrates why it’s crucial to: 1. Always visualize your data before interpreting correlation results 2. Check for outliers that might be driving the results 3. Compare Pearson and Spearman correlations—large discrepancies suggest outlier influence

Solutions for outliers: 1. Use Spearman correlation instead (robust to outliers) 2. Investigate outliers—are they data entry errors or legitimate extreme values? 3. If legitimate, consider reporting results both with and without the outlier 4. Report both Pearson and Spearman for comparison

Choosing the Right Statistical Test

Decision Guide

Selecting the appropriate statistical test depends on your research question and the types of variables involved. Here is a comprehensive guide covering the tests we have learned:

Guide to Selecting Statistical Tests
Research Question Statistical Test Healthcare Example R Function
Compare one group mean to a known value One-sample t-test Is our average LOS different from the national benchmark? t.test(x, mu = value)
Compare means of two independent groups Independent-samples t-test Do satisfaction scores differ between two hospitals? t.test(x, y) or t.test(y ~ group)
Compare means of same subjects at two times Paired-samples t-test Did scores improve after training? t.test(x, y, paired = TRUE)
Compare means of three or more groups One-way ANOVA Do satisfaction scores differ among three departments? aov(y ~ group)
Test association between two categorical variables Chi-square test (Crosstabulation) Is insurance type associated with readmission? chisq.test(table(x, y))
Test linear association between two continuous variables Pearson correlation Is staffing level related to satisfaction scores? cor.test(x, y, method = ‘pearson’)
Test association with ordinal variables or outliers Spearman correlation Is pain rating related to mobility score? cor.test(x, y, method = ‘spearman’)

Quick Reference Flowchart

Use this mental flowchart when choosing a test:

  1. What type of variables do you have?
    • Two categorical → Chi-square test
    • Two continuous/ordinal → Correlation
    • One categorical (grouping) + one continuous (outcome) → t-test or ANOVA
  2. If comparing group means:
    • How many groups?
      • One group vs. standard → One-sample t-test
      • Two groups → t-test (independent or paired)
      • Three+ groups → ANOVA
  3. If examining correlation:
    • Both variables continuous and no outliers → Pearson
    • Ordinal data or outliers present → Spearman
  4. If testing categorical association:
    • All expected frequencies ≥ 5 → Chi-square
    • Any expected frequency < 5 → Fisher’s exact

Summary

Key Takeaways

Crosstabulation (Chi-Square Test)

  • Purpose: Test association between two categorical variables
  • Null hypothesis: The variables are independent (no association)
  • Key statistic: Chi-square (χ²) measures discrepancy between observed and expected frequencies
  • Decision rule: If p < 0.05, reject independence; conclude significant association
  • Use Fisher’s exact test when any expected cell frequency < 5

Correlation Analysis

  • Purpose: Test linear association between two continuous or ordinal variables
  • Correlation coefficient (r): Ranges from -1 to +1
    • Sign indicates direction (positive/negative)
    • Absolute value indicates strength
  • Pearson: For continuous variables with linear relationships
  • Spearman: For ordinal variables or when outliers are present
  • Critical reminder: Correlation ≠ Causation
