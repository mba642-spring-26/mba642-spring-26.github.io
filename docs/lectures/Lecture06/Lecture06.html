Introduction to ANOVA

Why ANOVA Matters?

We frequently need to compare outcomes across more than two groups. Let’s think about these scenarios:

  • Comparing patient satisfaction scores across five different hospital departments
  • Evaluating readmission rates among three different care coordination models
  • Assessing staff performance across four different training programs
  • Tracking patient outcomes at multiple time points during recovery

While t-tests are excellent for comparing two groups (independent-samples t-test) or two related measures such as pre- and post-intervention scores (paired t-test), they cannot handle these multi-group or multi-timepoint comparisons. This is where Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) becomes essential. One-way ANOVA extends the independent-samples t-test to three or more groups, and repeated-measures ANOVA extends the paired t-test to three or more time points or related conditions—all in a single test.

The Problem with Multiple t-tests

You might wonder why not just run multiple t-tests to compare each pair of groups? This approach is tempting but fundamentally flawed due to the familywise error rate problem.

Familywise Error Rate

Each time we conduct a statistical test at α = 0.05, we accept a 5% chance of making a Type I error (false positive; concluding there’s a difference when there isn’t one). When we perform multiple tests, these error rates increase.

The probability of Type I error across multiple tests is:

\[P(\text{at least one Type I error}) = 1 - (1 - \alpha)^k\]

Where \(k\) is the number of comparisons.

For example, with just 4 groups, you would need 6 pairwise t-tests. The familywise error rate becomes: \[1 - (0.95)^6 = 0.265 = 26.5\%\]

This means you have a 26.5% chance of finding at least one “significant” difference by pure chance, which is way far from the 5% we intended!

ANOVA Solves This Problem

ANOVA tests all groups simultaneously with a single test, maintaining the Type I error rate at our desired α level (in our class, 5%). If ANOVA finds a significant difference, we then use post-hoc tests with appropriate corrections to identify which specific groups differ.

Overview of ANOVA Types

This document covers two fundamental ANOVA designs:

ANOVA Type Purpose Example
One-Way ANOVA Compare means across 3+ independent groups Comparing satisfaction scores across 4 hospital departments
Repeated-Measures ANOVA Compare means across 3+ related measurements on the same subjects Tracking pain scores at baseline, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months

1. One-Way ANOVA

What is it?

A one-way ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) tests whether there are statistically significant differences among the means of three or more independent groups. The “one-way” refers to having one independent variable (factor) with multiple levels (groups).

ANOVA actually analyzes variance to make inferences about means. It works by partitioning the total variability in the data into:

  1. Between-group variance: Differences among group means
  2. Within-group variance: Differences among individuals within groups

If the between-group variance is substantially larger than the within-group variance, we conclude that the group means are significantly different.

Key Components

  • One independent variable (factor) with 3+ levels
  • Independent groups: Each observation belongs to only one group
  • Continuous dependent variable: The outcome measure

When to Use It

One-way ANOVA is appropriate whenever you need to compare means across three or more distinct, unrelated groups:

  • Comparing patient outcomes across different treatment protocols
  • Evaluating staff performance across multiple departments
  • Comparing operational metrics across several facilities
  • Assessing patient satisfaction across various service lines
  • Comparing cost efficiency across different care models

Scenario 1: Patient Satisfaction Across Hospital Departments

A hospital administrator wants to evaluate patient satisfaction across four departments: Emergency, Cardiology, Orthopedics, and Oncology. Each department has implemented different patient experience initiatives, and leadership wants to know if satisfaction levels differ significantly.

The Formula

The one-way ANOVA calculates an F-statistic:

\[F = \frac{\text{Between-group variance}}{\text{Within-group variance}} = \frac{MS_{between}}{MS_{within}}\]

Where:

  • Mean Square Between (\(MS_{between}\)): Average variance among group means \[MS_{between} = \frac{SS_{between}}{df_{between}} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k} n_j(\bar{X}_j - \bar{X}_{grand})^2}{k-1}\]

  • Mean Square Within (\(MS_{within}\)): Average variance within groups \[MS_{within} = \frac{SS_{within}}{df_{within}} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n_j}(X_{ij} - \bar{X}_j)^2}{N-k}\]

Where:

  • \(k\) = number of groups

  • \(n_j\) = sample size of group \(j\)

  • \(N\) = total sample size

  • \(\bar{X}_j\) = mean of group \(j\)

  • \(\bar{X}_{grand}\) = overall mean (grand mean)

Understanding the F-Statistic

The F-statistic is a ratio:

  • When variance between groups is larger than variance within groups, F-statistic will be large
  • When variance between groups is smaller than variance within groups, F-statistic will be small

Visualizing the F-Distribution under the Null Hypothesis

Hypotheses

  • Null hypothesis (\(H_0\)): All group means are equal (\(\mu_1 = \mu_2 = \mu_3 = \mu_4\))
  • Alternative hypothesis (\(H_A\)): At least one group mean is different from the others

Interpretation of the graph

  • The density plot shows the F-distribution under the null hypothesis.
  • The red line indicates the calculated F-statistic, and the shaded gray area indicates the rejection region.
  • The area to the right of the calculated F-statistic (under the curve) is the p-value.
  • If the F-statistic falls in the rejection region, we reject the null hypothesis and conclude that there are significant differences among the group means.
  • However, a significant ANOVA result does not tell us which specific groups differ from each other. To identify where the differences lie, we need to conduct post-hoc analysis while controlling for the familywise error rate.

Conducting One-Way ANOVA in R

As we now understand the basic concept of ANOVA, let’s conduct one-way ANOVA in R. In R, you can conduct a one-way ANOVA by using the aov(y ~ x, data = data) function.

# Perform one-way ANOVA
anova_result <- aov(score ~ department, data = satisfaction_data)
summary(anova_result)
            Df Sum Sq Mean Sq F value  Pr(>F)   
department   3  4.451  1.4838   5.143 0.00272 **
Residuals   76 21.928  0.2885                   
---
Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

Interpreting the ANOVA Table

  • Df (Degrees of Freedom):

    • Between groups: \(k - 1 = 4 - 1 = 3\)
    • Within groups: \(N - k = 80 - 4 = 76\)

  • Sum Sq (Sum of Squares): The amount of variation attributed to each source. The factor row (e.g., department) shows variation due to group differences, while the Residuals row shows variation within groups (individual differences not explained by group membership)

  • Mean Sq (Mean Square): Sum of Squares divided by degrees of freedom

  • F value: The test statistic (ratio of between-group to within-group variance)

  • Pr(>F): The p-value (the probability of observing this F-value if all group means were truly equal)

Conclusion from ANOVA

With F = 5.14 and p < 0.05, we reject the null hypothesis. There are statistically significant differences in patient satisfaction among the four departments.

But which departments differ from each other? The ANOVA tells us that differences exist but not where they are. For this, we need post-hoc tests.

Post-Hoc Tests: Tukey’s HSD

When ANOVA is significant, we use post-hoc tests to identify which specific groups differ. Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test compares all possible pairs while controlling the familywise error rate.

What is Tukey’s HSD?

Tukey’s HSD is designed to solve a specific problem: How do we compare all pairs of groups while maintaining our overall Type I error rate at α = 0.05?

Key features:

  1. Tests all pairwise comparisons: For k groups, it tests all \(\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\) possible pairs

    • 4 groups → 6 comparisons
    • 5 groups → 10 comparisons

  2. Controls familywise error rate: Your overall chance of making at least one Type I error stays at 5% across ALL comparisons (not 5% per comparison)

  3. Uses the studentized range distribution: More appropriate than t-distribution for multiple comparisons

The HSD critical value:

\[HSD = q_{\alpha, k, df_{error}} \times \sqrt{\frac{MS_{within}}{n}}\]

Where:

  • \(q_{\alpha, k, df_{error}}\) = critical value from studentized range distribution
  • \(MS_{within}\) = mean square error from ANOVA
  • \(n\) = sample size per group
  • \(k\) = number of groups

If the absolute difference between two group means exceeds HSD, that difference is statistically significant.

Conducting Tukey’s HSD in R Using emmeans

  • Now let’s use the emmeans package, which is the modern standard for post-hoc testing. This gives us the estimated marginal means (EMMs), which are the adjusted means for each group.

  • Then, we need to specify the pairwise comparisons using the pairs() function and the adjust = "tukey" argument to apply Tukey’s HSD adjustment.

# Step 1: Calculate estimated marginal means
emm_dept <- emmeans(anova_result, ~ department)
emm_dept

 Orthopedics   7.75 0.12 76     7.52     7.99

Confidence level used: 0.95

Interpreting Tukey’s HSD Output

Each row in the output shows a pairwise comparison:

  • contrast: Which two groups are being compared
  • estimate: Difference between group means
  • SE: Standard error of the difference
  • df: Degrees of freedom
  • t.ratio: Test statistic
  • p.value: Adjusted p-value (controlling for multiple comparisons)

Decision rule:

  • If p.value < 0.05, those groups are significantly different

Example 2: Comparing Care Coordination Models

  • A health system wants to evaluate three different care coordination models for managing patients with chronic conditions. They measure 30-day readmission rates (%) across facilities using each model.

  • Studies have shown that different care coordination approaches produce varying outcomes, with community health worker interventions and transition care coordinator models demonstrating significant reductions in hospital readmissions. Model A: Nurse-Led Telephone Follow-Up

  • Registered nurses call patients within 48 hours of discharge
  • Phone-based medication reconciliation and education
  • Identification of red flags or concerning symptoms
  • Lowest resource intensity but limited ability to assess home environment
  • Relies on patient self-report

Model B: Community Health Worker (CHW) Home Visits

  • Trained community health workers conduct in-person home visits within 72 hours

  • Can connect patients to community resources (food, transportation, social services)

  • Higher resource intensity than phone calls

  • Multidisciplinary team (nurse, pharmacist, social worker) coordinates care
  • Patients receive remote monitoring devices (blood pressure, weight, pulse oximetry)
  • Daily automated data transmission to care team
  • Algorithm-driven alerts trigger proactive interventions
  • Weekly team huddles to review high-risk patients

2. Repeated-Measures ANOVA

  • So far, we have compared means across independent groups (e.g., different departments, training programs, or care models) where each observation belongs to only one group.

  • Now we turn to situations where the same subjects or patients are measured multiple times. This requires a different approach that accounts for the correlation between repeated observations on the same individual.

What is it?

  • A repeated-measures ANOVA (also called within-subjects ANOVA) tests whether there are significant differences across three or more related measurements on the same respondents. This design is the extension of the paired t-test to situations involving more than two time points, measurements, or conditions.

  • The key advantage of repeated-measures designs is that each respondent serves as their own control, which reduces error variance and increases statistical power. By tracking the same individuals over time, we eliminate individual differences as a source of variability.

When to Use It

Repeated-measures ANOVA is appropriate for:

  • Longitudinal studies: Tracking patients at multiple time points
    • Pain scores at baseline (month 0), 1 month, 3 months, 6 months
    • HbA1c levels across quarterly clinic visits
  • Within-subject experiments: Testing same subjects under different conditions
    • Response to different medication dosages
    • Performance under different shift schedules
  • Quality improvement tracking: Monitoring metrics over multiple periods
    • Hospital safety scores across quarters
    • Patient satisfaction before, during, and after initiative implementation

Scenario 1: Diabetes Management Program

Background and Context

A healthcare organization implements a comprehensive diabetes management program for patients with poorly controlled Type 2 diabetes. The program includes:

  • Monthly nurse educator visits for medication management and lifestyle coaching
  • Quarterly endocrinologist consultations for treatment plan adjustments
  • Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) with remote monitoring by care team
  • Nutritionist consultations for personalized meal planning
  • Group support sessions for peer learning and motivation

The Outcome Measure: HbA1c

What is HbA1c?

  • Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) measures average blood glucose over the past 2-3 months
  • Reported as a percentage (e.g., 7.5%)
  • Clinical targets:
    • Normal: < 5.7%
    • Prediabetes: 5.7-6.4%
    • Diabetes: ≥ 6.5%
    • Treatment goal: < 7.5%
  • Why it matters: Each 1% reduction in HbA1c is associated with significant reductions in diabetes complications (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy)

Study Design

The organization tracks HbA1c levels for 25 enrolled patients at four time points:

  • Baseline: Before program enrollment (typical range: 8-10% for poorly controlled diabetes)
  • 3 months: Initial follow-up (early response to intervention)
  • 6 months: Mid-program (sustained adherence check)
  • 12 months: Program completion (long-term effectiveness)

Why Use Repeated-Measures ANOVA?

This is a perfect scenario for repeated-measures ANOVA because:

  1. Same patients tracked over time: Each patient serves as their own control
  2. Multiple time points: Four measurements (more than two)
  3. Reduces individual variability: Some patients naturally have higher HbA1c than others; repeated measures accounts for this
  4. Efficient use of resources: We need only 25 patients instead of 100 (25 per group × 4 groups) if we used one-way ANOVA
  5. Longitudinal question: We want to know if HbA1c changes over time within the same individuals

Research Questions

  1. Does HbA1c change significantly over the course of the 12-month program?

  2. If significant:

  • At which time points do we see significant improvements?
  • Do patients reach the clinical target of < 7.5% by 12 months?
  • Is the improvement sustained or does it plateau?

The Formula

Repeated-measures ANOVA partitions variance differently than one-way ANOVA to account for the correlation between measurements on the same subject:

\[F = \frac{MS_{time}}{MS_{error}}\]

Where:

  • \(MS_{time}\) = Mean square for the time (or condition) effect
  • \(MS_{error}\) = Mean square for error (residual variation after removing both subject and time effects)

Formulas for Sum of Squares Components

To calculate the variance components in repeated-measures ANOVA, we need these formulas:

0. Total Sum of Squares (SS_total):

\[SS_{total} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{t} (X_{ij} - \bar{X}_{grand})^2\]

Where:

  • \(X_{ij}\) = observation for subject \(i\) at time point \(j\)
  • \(\bar{X}_{grand}\) = overall grand mean (across all subjects and all time points)
  • \(n\) = number of subjects
  • \(t\) = number of time points

This measures total variation in all observations from the grand mean. This is the “starting point” that we partition into components.

1. Sum of Squares for Time Effect (SS_time):

\[SS_{time} = n \sum_{j=1}^{t} (\bar{X}_j - \bar{X}_{grand})^2\]

Where:

  • \(n\) = number of subjects (the weighting factor; each time point mean is based on \(n\) observations)
  • \(t\) = number of time points
  • \(\bar{X}_j\) = mean at time point \(j\) (averaging across all subjects)
  • \(\bar{X}_{grand}\) = overall grand mean (across all subjects and all time points)

This measures how much the time point means differ from the overall mean. We multiply by \(n\) because each time point’s mean represents \(n\) subjects.

2. Sum of Squares Between-Subjects (SS_subjects):

\[SS_{subjects} = t \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\bar{X}_i - \bar{X}_{grand})^2\]

Where:

  • \(t\) = number of time points (the weighting factor; each subject’s mean is based on \(t\) observations)
  • \(\bar{X}_i\) = mean for subject \(i\) (averaging across all time points)

This measures how much individual subjects differ from the overall mean. We multiply by \(t\) because each subject’s mean represents \(t\) observations. This component gets removed from the error term, which is the key advantage of repeated-measures designs!

3. Sum of Squares Error (SS_error):

\[SS_{error} = SS_{total} - SS_{subjects} - SS_{time}\]

Or, calculated directly:

\[SS_{error} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{t} (X_{ij} - \bar{X}_i - \bar{X}_j + \bar{X}_{grand})^2\]

Where:

  • \(X_{ij}\) = observation for subject \(i\) at time \(j\)
  • \(\bar{X}_i\) = mean for subject \(i\)
  • \(\bar{X}_j\) = mean for time \(j\)
  • \(\bar{X}_{grand}\) = grand mean

This measures random variation left over after removing both subject differences and time effects.

Notice that \(SS_{error}\) is calculated by subtracting out both subject means AND time means. This is why it’s smaller than one-way ANOVA error (we’ve removed the between-subjects variance!)

Calculating the F-Statistic

The F-statistic tests whether the time effect is larger than random error:

\[F = \frac{MS_{time}}{MS_{error}} = \frac{SS_{time}/df_{time}}{SS_{error}/df_{error}}\]

Where:

  • \(df_{time} = t - 1\) (number of time points minus 1)
  • \(df_{error} = (n-1) \times (t-1)\) (subjects - 1) × (time points - 1)

Hypotheses:

  • \(H_0\): All time point means are equal (\(\mu_{baseline} = \mu_{3mo} = \mu_{6mo} = \mu_{12mo}\))
  • \(H_A\): At least one time point mean is different from the others

Interpretation:

  • Similar to one-way ANOVA, as the variation across time points is getting larger (vs. smaller), the F-statistic is getting larger (vs. smaller).
  • If the F-statistic is large enough, we reject the null hypothesis and conclude that there is a significant change in HbA1c over time.

Conducting Repeated-Measures ANOVA in R

  • We can also use the aov() function to perform a repeated-measures ANOVA.
  • For repeated-measures ANOVA, we additionally need to specify the error term to account for the within-subjects design.
  • The error term is specified as Error(patient_id/time), which tells R that each patient (patient_id) is measured repeatedly across time. This partitions the error into between-subjects and within-subjects components, so the time effect is tested appropriately.

Hypotheses:

  • \(H_0\): Mean HbA1c is equal across all four time points (\(\mu_{baseline} = \mu_{3mo} = \mu_{6mo} = \mu_{12mo}\))
  • \(H_A\): At least one time point’s mean HbA1c differs from the others
# Repeated-measures ANOVA
# Error(patient_id/time) specifies that time is within subjects
rm_anova <- aov(hba1c ~ time + Error(patient_id/time), data = diabetes_data)
summary(rm_anova)

Summary

Key Takeaways

Test Purpose Key Points
One-Way ANOVA Compare means across 3+ independent groups Single factor, between-subjects design
Repeated-Measures ANOVA Compare means across 3+ related measurements Same subjects, within-subjects design
Post-Hoc Tests Identify which specific groups differ Use after significant ANOVA, adjusts for multiple comparisons

  • One-way ANOVA enables evidence-based comparisons across multiple departments, facilities, treatment protocols, or patient populations simultaneously while controlling Type I error.

  • Repeated-measures ANOVA is essential for evaluating longitudinal programs and interventions, tracking patient outcomes over time, measuring the effectiveness of quality improvement initiatives, or assessing staff development programs.

  • Post-hoc tests ensure that when you identify “significant differences,” you can understand exactly where those differences lie, enabling targeted action rather than broad, unfocused interventions.
