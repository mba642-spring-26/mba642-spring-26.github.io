Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish between populations, samples, and sub-populations
  • Identify appropriate distributions for different healthcare outcomes
  • Explain conditional vs. marginal means
  • Understand overdispersion and between-individual heterogeneity
  • Critically interpret p-values and hypothesis tests
  • Recognize common pitfalls in statistical inference

This chapter provides essential statistical foundations for analyzing healthcare data. Emphasize that healthcare outcomes rarely follow normal distributions, which motivates much of the material.

Populations and Samples

Population: The complete group we want to learn about

  • Well-defined (e.g., all Hiroshima atomic bomb survivors who survived 1+ years)
  • Can be hypothetical (e.g., all breast cancer patients who have/will undergo mastectomy)

Sample: A subset of the population used for inference

  • Random samples enable quantification of uncertainty
  • Many healthcare studies use convenience samples or complete censuses

Sub-population: A population subset satisfying specific criteria

  • Defined by fixed characteristics (e.g., men under 20 in Hiroshima cohort)
  • Regression models estimate means for sub-populations defined by covariates

Emphasize that representativeness is always a concern, regardless of how samples are obtained. Discuss how this relates to external validity.

Random Variables in Healthcare

Healthcare outcomes are random variables that vary across individuals:

  • Binary: Re-admission after surgery (Yes/No)
  • Categorical: Treatment type (surgery/radiation/hormonal)
  • Count: Number of outpatient visits, prescriptions filled
  • Continuous: Medical expenditures, length of stay

Notation: - Capital letters (X, Y) for generic/theoretical variables - Lowercase letters (x, y) for specific observed values

Give concrete examples from students’ experience. Ask: What healthcare outcomes have they encountered? What types of variables are these?

Dependent vs. Independent Variables

Research Question Determines the Relationship

  • Dependent variable (Y): Outcome or response
  • Independent variable (X): Predictor or covariate

Example: Exercise and insomnia

  • Exercise may reduce insomnia → Exercise is X, insomnia is Y
  • Insomnia may reduce exercise ability → Insomnia is X, exercise is Y
  • Both may be true (feedback loop) → Modeling challenge!

Key principle: Variables that co-evolve should often both be considered outcomes

Discuss the importance of conceptual models (like Andersen-Newman) in sorting out variable relationships. Medical expenditures and hospitalizations example from the text.

Statistical Distributions

A distribution describes the probability that a variable takes any value in its range

For categorical variables: Finite set of probabilities (sum to 1)

For continuous variables: Probability density function (PDF)

  • Area under PDF over an interval = probability of value in that interval
  • Cumulative distribution function (CDF) = probability ≤ specified value

Key summaries: - Mean (expected value): Most typical value, influenced by extremes - Median (50th percentile): Center of distribution, robust to outliers

Healthcare data is often right-skewed, so median may be more representative than mean. Show the MEPS expenditure histogram from Figure 2.1.

MEPS Total Expenditure Example

  • Mean: $18,145
  • Median: $9,664
  • Extreme right skew with long tail
  • Mean > Median indicates right skewness

This is typical of healthcare expenditure data. A few high-cost users drive up the mean substantially above the median.

Parameters and Models

Parameters: Greek letters (μ, σ, α, β) that specify a distribution

  • Normal distribution: mean μ, variance σ²
  • Parametric methods: specify family, estimate parameters

Models: Assumptions and mathematical specifications for analysis

  • Rarely unique - multiple models may address same question
  • Model choice affects both estimates and their standard errors
  • What constitutes a “good model” is a recurring theme

Emphasize that model assumptions matter greatly. Healthcare outcomes often violate normality assumption, motivating specialized distributions.

Estimation and Inference

Estimation: Using sample data to learn about population

  • Sample mean estimates population mean
  • Sample median estimates population median

Statistical Inference: Using estimates to answer research questions

  • Combines estimates with their uncertainty (standard errors)
  • Hypothesis tests are one tool for inference

Standard Error (SE): Quantifies variation over repeated samples

  • Higher SE → greater expected variation
  • Decreases with larger sample size
  • Depends on the model used

Different models can yield similar point estimates but very different standard errors, affecting inference. Preview the two-part model discussion from Chapter 6.

This table illustrates both conditional and marginal means. Each expenditure is conditional on health status.

Conditional vs. Marginal Means (cont.)

Conditional mean: \(E(Y | X = x)\) - mean for specific sub-population

  • \(E(Y | \text{Excellent}) = \$1,957\)
  • \(E(Y | \text{Poor}) = \$22,619\)

Marginal mean: \(E(Y)\) - weighted average across all sub-populations

\[E(Y) = 1957(0.34) + 3729(0.29) + 6183(0.25) + 11387(0.10) + 22619(0.03)\] \[= \$5,110\]

Lower than highest categories because healthier (lower-cost) people are more common!

This is a crucial concept for regression. Marginal mean averages over the distribution of X. Emphasize that weights matter.

Bernoulli and Binomial Distributions

Bernoulli: Binary outcome (0 or 1)

  • Parameter: \(p = P(Y = 1)\)
  • Mean: \(E(Y) = p\)
  • Variance: \(Var(Y) = p(1-p)\)
  • Variance depends on mean!

Binomial: Sum of n independent Bernoulli variables

  • Parameters: n (number of trials), p (success probability)
  • Mean: \(np\)
  • Variance: \(np(1-p)\)

Example: Hospital re-admissions after surgery

Binary prediction is a classification problem. Statistical learning has different algorithms for this (Chapter 10). Emphasize that binomial requires independence - clustering violates this.

Binomial Distribution

Shape depends on both n and p. As n increases, distribution becomes more bell-shaped.

Show how the distribution changes with parameters. When p=0.5 and n is large, it approaches normal distribution (Central Limit Theorem preview).

Multinomial Distribution

Generalization of binomial for multiple categories (K > 2)

Examples:

  • Treatment choice: surgery/radiation/hormonal (3 unordered categories)
  • Health status: excellent/very good/good/fair/poor (5 ordered categories)

Parameters: K-1 independent probabilities (must sum to 1)

Analysis Goal: Identify factors predicting probability of each category

  • How does diabetes affect \(P(Y = k)\) for each health status category k?

Ordered vs. unordered categories require different modeling approaches. Can treat ordered as continuous or use ordinal regression methods.

Poisson Distribution for Counts

Basic distribution for count data (0, 1, 2, 3, …)

Assumptions: - Events occur at constant rate - Memoryless (past doesn’t affect future probability)

Parameter: λ (mean rate)

Key property: Mean = Variance = λ

Highly restrictive for healthcare data!

Show Figure 2.3. The equal mean-variance assumption is almost always violated in practice. Healthcare counts typically show overdispersion.

Poisson Distribution

As λ increases, distribution becomes more symmetric and bell-shaped.

Overdispersion in Healthcare Data

Observed MEPS outpatient visits vs. Simulated Poisson

  • Observed: Mean = 1.5, Variance = 24 (16× mean!)
  • More zeros and longer right tail than Poisson predicts

This is typical of healthcare utilization data. Poisson assumption is too restrictive. Need alternative models.

Negative Binomial Distribution

Accommodates overdispersion: Variance > Mean

Mechanism: Between-individual heterogeneity

  • Each person has their own latent “frailty” or “susceptibility”
  • Some rarely seek care (low frailty), others frequently (high frailty)
  • Individual’s conditional mean: \(\mu r_i\) (where \(r_i\) is personal frailty)

Result: If frailties follow gamma distribution with mean 1, variance α - Marginal distribution of Y is negative binomial - Mean: μ - Variance: \(\mu(1 + \alpha\mu)\) > \(\mu\)

This latent heterogeneity concept recurs in random effects and frailty models. Emphasize that it’s a specific mechanism for overdispersion.

Normal Distribution

Symmetric, bell-shaped distribution

Parameters: mean μ, standard deviation σ

Key properties: - 67% of observations within 1 SD of mean - 95% within 2 SDs - Values >2 SDs away are “atypical”

Standardization: \(Z = (Y - \mu)/\sigma\) has mean 0, SD 1

Show Figure 2.5. Most healthcare outcomes are NOT normal, but the normal is crucial because of the Central Limit Theorem - sample means are approximately normal.

Normal Distribution

Different σ values show different spreads. The distribution is completely determined by μ and σ.

Central Limit Theorem

Key insight: Even if data are non-normal, sample means are approximately normal

  • Distribution of sample mean \(\bar{Y}\) approaches normal as n increases
  • True regardless of the distribution of Y itself
  • Enables hypothesis testing based on normal approximation

Crucial distinction: - Population data distribution: Often non-normal in healthcare - Sample estimate distribution: Approximately normal

This is why we can use normal-based inference even with skewed healthcare data. But this applies to the sampling distribution of estimates, not the data itself.

Gamma Distribution

Flexible distribution for non-negative, continuous, right-skewed outcomes

Parameters: α (shape), β (rate)

  • Mean: α/β
  • Variance: α/β²

Can accommodate various shapes from highly skewed to more symmetric

Commonly used for medical expenditures

Show Figure 2.6. The gamma is quite flexible and can match many right-skewed distributions. The variance can be greater or less than the mean.

Gamma Distribution

As α increases (for fixed β), the distribution becomes more symmetric and bell-shaped.

Lognormal Distribution

Y has lognormal distribution if log(Y) has normal distribution

If \(\log(Y) \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\), then:

\[E(Y) = \exp\left(\mu + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right) \neq \exp(\mu)\]

Critical point: Order of operations matters!

  • \(E[\log(Y)] \neq \log[E(Y)]\)
  • Retransformation is not straightforward

Usage: Any log-transformed regression implicitly assumes lognormal outcomes

This retransformation issue is a major challenge in cost regression (Chapter 6). Show Table 2.2 from MEPS data.

Retransformation Problem

MEPS 2017 Example

Expense Type Direct Mean \(E[\log(Y)]\) \(\exp\{E[\log(Y)]\}\)
Total $5,995 7.27 $1,433
Outpatient $2,767 6.59 $728
Inpatient $18,145 9.10 $8,967

Cannot simply exponentiate the mean of logged values!

Correction factor needed: \(\exp(\sigma^2/2)\)

This is a common mistake. Emphasize that quantiles (like median) do transform directly, which motivates quantile regression.

Lognormal Distribution

Right-skewed with heavy right tail, suitable for expenditure data

Different σ values control the degree of skewness. Larger σ → heavier tail.

Hypothesis Testing Framework

Purpose: Reach decision about hypothesis while quantifying error risk

Structure: - Null hypothesis \(H_0\): typically what we want to disprove - Alternative \(H_A\): what we believe - Test statistic from sample data - P-value: probability of observed data (or more extreme) if \(H_0\) is true

Decision: - Reject \(H_0\) if p-value is small - Cannot “accept” \(H_0\), only “fail to reject”

Emphasize the one-way nature. Absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence. This asymmetry is fundamental to the framework.

What P-values Actually Mean

P-value: Probability of observing data as or more extreme than what we saw, assuming \(H_0\) is true

Common misconception: P-value = probability that \(H_0\) is true ❌

Correct interpretation: P-value quantifies how inconsistent the data are with \(H_0\)

  • Low p-value → data unlikely under \(H_0\) → reject \(H_0\)
  • High p-value → data not inconsistent with \(H_0\) → cannot conclude anything

This is the most common misinterpretation. P-value is a statement about the data, not about the hypothesis. Many published papers get this wrong.

Problems with P-value Thresholds

Traditional “rule”: p < 0.05 = “statistically significant”

Problems:

  1. Large samples: Trivial effects can have tiny p-values
    • Effect size ≈ 0 but p < 0.001
  2. Small samples: Important effects may have large p-values
    • Low statistical power
  3. Multiple comparisons: Error rate inflates
    • 5 tests: P(at least 1 false positive) = 0.23
    • 10 tests: P(at least 1 false positive) = 0.40
  4. Invites p-hacking: Manipulation to achieve p < 0.05

ASA statement (2016) and many journals now discourage bright-line thresholds. Focus on effect sizes and confidence intervals instead.

P-hacking and Researcher Degrees of Freedom

P-hacking: Trying multiple analyses until finding p < 0.05

Methods: - Selective reporting of results - Choosing convenient exclusion criteria - Dichotomizing continuous variables at chosen cut-points - Dropping certain missing data patterns - Choosing specific statistical tests

Solution: Pre-specify analysis plan, report all analyses conducted

Best practice: Focus on effect sizes and practical significance

With large healthcare databases, this is especially tempting and problematic. Reproducibility crisis in science partly stems from this.

Multiple Comparisons Problem

Example: Testing 10 covariates individually for association with expenditures

  • Each test at α = 0.05
  • P(at least 1 false positive) = \(1 - (0.95)^{10} = 0.40\)

Solutions:

  1. Bonferroni correction: Test each at α/k level
  2. False discovery rate: Control proportion of false positives
  3. Pre-specification: Develop strong conceptual model first

In genomic studies with thousands of tests, this is critical. But even with 5-10 tests, the problem is real. Conceptual models help limit number of tests.

Best Practices for Inference

Do: - Report effect sizes with confidence intervals - Consider practical significance alongside statistical significance - Acknowledge uncertainty in p-values themselves - Pre-specify hypotheses when possible - Report all analyses conducted - Consider multiple comparison adjustments

Don’t: - Treat p = 0.049 differently from p = 0.051 - Conclude \(H_0\) is true from large p-value - Interpret p-value as P(\(H_0\) is true) - Hide analyses that didn’t achieve p < 0.05

Growing recognition in scientific community of these limitations. Many journals now require pre-registration of analysis plans. Emphasize reproducibility.

Key Takeaways

  1. Healthcare outcomes require specialized distributions (not just normal)

  2. Understand overdispersion and between-individual heterogeneity

  3. Conditional vs. marginal estimation is fundamental to regression

  4. Transformation requires careful retransformation

  5. P-values are widely misunderstood - focus on effect sizes

  6. Standard errors matter as much as point estimates

  7. Model assumptions affect both estimates and inference

These concepts form the foundation for regression methods in subsequent chapters. Emphasize that appropriate distribution choice is driven by outcome type.
