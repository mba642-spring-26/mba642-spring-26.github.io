Published

January 1, 2026

1 Introduction to Conjoint Analysis

1.1 What is Conjoint Analysis?

Conjoint analysis is a survey-based statistical technique used to understand how people make decisions when facing trade-offs between multiple attributes of a product or service.

The core idea is simple: in real life, we rarely get everything we want. When choosing a health insurance plan, for example, we might prefer both low premiums AND low deductibles, but plans with low premiums often come with high deductibles. Conjoint analysis helps us understand how people navigate these trade-offs.

The term “conjoint” comes from “considered jointly” (we’re studying how attributes are evaluated together rather than in isolation). This is crucial because asking someone “Do you prefer low cost?” will always get a “yes,” but asking them to choose between a low-cost option with long wait times vs. a high-cost option with short wait times reveals their true priorities.

1.2 Why Use Conjoint Analysis in Healthcare Management?

Healthcare managers face constant decisions about service design:

  • Insurance plan design: What combination of premium, deductible, and coverage will attract people?
  • Clinic operations: Should we prioritize shorter wait times or extended hours?
  • Telehealth services: Do patients value video consultations enough to pay more for them?
  • Hospital services: What amenities matter most to patients choosing where to receive care?

Conjoint analysis answers these questions by quantifying preferences. Instead of relying on intuition or simple surveys (e.g., “Would you like shorter wait times?”), conjoint analysis reveals how much each feature actually matters when people must make realistic trade-offs.

1.3 Key Outputs from Conjoint Analysis

By using conjoint analysis, we can get several valuable insights:

1.3.1 1. Part-Worth Utilities

Part-worth utilities (also called “part-worths” or “utility scores”) are the building blocks of conjoint analysis. They represent the value or satisfaction that a person derives from a specific level of an attribute.

What is “utility”? In this context, “utility” is an economics term meaning satisfaction or value. Think of it as a numeric score representing how much someone values something. Higher utility = more preferred, lower utility = less preferred.

Think of it this way: every product or service is a bundle of attributes. A health insurance plan is a bundle of (premium, deductible, network size, etc.). The total utility (satisfaction) a person gets from a plan is the sum of the part-worth utilities for each attribute level in that plan.

For example, if the part-worth for “Large Network” is +3 and for “Small Network” is 0, then having a large network adds 3 utility units to the overall plan value. These numbers are relative (they only make sense in comparison to other levels of the same attribute, not stand alone).

1.3.2 2. Attribute Importance

Once we have part-worth utilities, we can calculate how important each attribute is relative to others. This is done by looking at the range of utilities within each attribute (highest level minus lowest level) and comparing these ranges across attributes.

For example, if premium ranges from 0 to 5 utility units and network size ranges from 0 to 2 units, then premium has a larger impact on preferences and is therefore more “important.”

1.3.3 3. Willingness-to-Pay (WTP)

When price or cost is one of the attributes, we can convert utility differences into dollar values. This tells us how much extra a person would be willing to pay for an improvement in another attribute.

For example, if people would pay $50 more per month for a large network versus a small network, this helps managers decide whether investing in network expansion is worthwhile.

1.3.4 4. Market Share Prediction

By knowing the utilities for each attribute level, we can predict what percentage of people would choose one option over another. This is invaluable for forecasting demand and competitive positioning.

1.4 Two Main Approaches

In this class, we will cover the two most common conjoint methods:

1.4.1 Rating-Based Conjoint

  • What respondents do: Rate each profile on a scale (e.g., 1-10)
  • Analysis approach: Linear regression (OLS)
  • Advantages: Simple to understand and implement; builds on familiar regression
  • Limitations: Respondents may rate all options similarly, not revealing true trade-offs

1.4.2 Choice-Based Conjoint (Discrete Choice Experiment)

  • What respondents do: Choose their preferred option from a set
  • Analysis approach: Multinomial logistic regression
  • Advantages: Mimics real-world decisions; forces explicit trade-offs
  • Limitations: Requires more data preparation and unfamiliar statistical method

Both methods ultimately produce the same types of outputs (part-worths, importance, WTP, market share), but choice-based conjoint is generally preferred in academic research and practice because it better reflects actual decision-making behavior (Louviere et al., 2000).

Now that you understand what conjoint analysis is and why it’s valuable in healthcare management, let’s work through a practical example. We’ll use one comprehensive case study that demonstrates both rating-based and choice-based approaches, allowing us to see how the methods compare and when you might use each one.

2 Scenario: Health Insurance Plan Selection

2.1 Context and Background

One of the most studied applications of conjoint analysis in healthcare management is understanding how employees choose health insurance plans during open enrollment periods. This is a high-stakes decision for both employees (who will live with the plan for a year) and employers (who subsidize the plans).

Research has shown that employees often struggle with this decision due to the complexity of plan attributes. Studies by Sinaiko and Hirth (2011) in the Journal of Health Economics found that many employees make “dominated choices” (selecting plans that are objectively worse than alternatives on all dimensions). Understanding which attributes drive choices can help employers design better communication strategies and plan offerings.

Our example uses three key attributes that consistently appear in health plan conjoint studies:

Attribute Level 1 Level 2 Why It Matters
Monthly Premium $200 $300 Direct, visible cost paid every month
Annual Deductible $500 $1,500 Amount paid before insurance kicks in
Network Size Large Small Access to preferred doctors/hospitals

2.2 Rating-Based Conjoint Analysis

2.2.1 Conceptual Overview

In rating-based conjoint analysis, we show respondents hypothetical profiles one at a time and ask them to rate how attractive each option is. For example:

Profile 1: Monthly premium $200, Deductible $1,500, Small network How likely would you be to choose this plan? (Rate 1-10)

Profile 2: Monthly premium $300, Deductible $500, Large network How likely would you be to choose this plan? (Rate 1-10)

A respondent might rate Profile 1 as 4/10 and Profile 2 as 7/10.

By collecting ratings for multiple profiles across many respondents, we can use regression analysis to decompose these ratings into the contribution of each attribute. This works because we have systematically varied the attributes across profiles.

2.2.2 The Statistical Model

Rating-based conjoint uses ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, the same technique you’ve learned in previous courses. The model assumes that a person’s rating is a linear combination of the utilities they assign to each attribute level:

\[\text{Rating} = \beta_0 + \beta_1(\text{Premium}) + \beta_2(\text{Deductible}) + \beta_3(\text{Network}) + \varepsilon\]

Where:

  • \(\beta_0\) is the intercept (baseline rating)
  • \(\beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3\) are the part-worth utilities for each attribute
  • \(\varepsilon\) is random error (individual variation, measurement error, etc.)

The coefficients we estimate ARE the part-worth utilities. They tell us how much each unit change in an attribute affects the rating.

Important note about coding variables

In conjoint analysis, we can code attributes in different ways depending on their nature:

  • Continuous variables (e.g., premium in dollars, deductible in dollars): Treated as numeric values. The coefficient tells us the utility change per unit (e.g., per dollar).
  • Categorical variables (e.g., network size): Coded as dummy variables (0 or 1). The coefficient represents the utility difference between categories (e.g., Large network vs. Small network).

In our insurance example, Premium and Deductible are continuous (dollar amounts), while Network Size is categorical (Large vs. Small).

2.2.6 Calculating Attribute Importance

Attribute importance tells us which features have the biggest impact on preferences. The calculation:

  1. For each attribute, calculate the utility range: difference between highest and lowest part-worth values
  2. Sum all the ranges
  3. Express each range as a percentage of the total

Why does a larger range mean more important?

An attribute’s range tells us how much it can change someone’s overall evaluation. If premium has a utility range of 5 points and network has a range of 2 points, switching from worst to best premium gives you 5 utility points, while switching network levels only gives 2 points. The attribute with the larger range has more power to influence preferences.

CRITICAL CAVEAT: These importance scores are completely dependent on the range of levels you test. Testing premium of $200 vs. $201 would make premium appear unimportant, while $200 vs. $500 would make it appear very important. Importance scores do NOT tell us which attributes are “objectively most important.” They tell us which attributes have the most impact GIVEN THE SPECIFIC LEVELS YOU TESTED. Always choose attribute levels that reflect realistic market variation.

Let’s get regression coefficients and calculate importance:

# 1. Extract coefficients
coef_ins <- coef(model_rating_ins)

# 2. Calculate utility ranges for each attribute
range_premium <- abs(coef_ins["premium"] * (300 - 200))  # $100 difference in monthly premium
range_deductible <- abs(coef_ins["deductible"] * (1500 - 500))  # $1000 difference in deductible
range_network <- abs(coef_ins["network_large"] * 1)  # network size: Large vs Small

# 3. Total range
total_range <- range_premium + range_deductible + range_network

# 4. Calculate importance as percentage
importance_rating_ins <- data.frame(
  attribute = c("Monthly Premium", "Annual Deductible", "Network Size"),
  utility_range = c(range_premium, range_deductible, range_network),
  importance_pct = c(range_premium, range_deductible, range_network) / total_range * 100
)

2.2.7 Calculating Willingness-to-Pay (WTP)

Willingness-to-pay converts abstract utility values into concrete dollar amounts. This is possible because we included a price attribute (monthly premium) in our study.

The Logic:

If we know (1) how much utility a person gets from each attribute and (2) how much utility they lose per dollar of cost, we can calculate the dollar amount that would exactly offset any utility gain.

For example, if the utility of a large network is +0.6 points, and the utility loss per dollar of premium is -0.012 points, then: 0.6 / 0.012 = $50. This means respondents value a large network as much as they would value a $50 reduction in monthly premium.

The Formula: \[\text{WTP} = -\frac{\beta_{\text{attribute}}}{\beta_{\text{price}}}\]

WTP Calculations:

From our regression model:

  • Premium coefficient: -0.0116 utility points per dollar
  • Network coefficient: 0.6465 utility points for large network
  • Deductible coefficient: -0.0008 utility points per dollar

WTP for Large Network (vs. Small Network):

\[\text{WTP}_{\text{network}} = -\frac{0.6465}{-0.0116} = \$55.73\]

Interpretation: Enrollees would be willing to pay an additional $55.73 per month to have access to a large provider network instead of a small network.

WTP for $1,000 Lower Deductible:

For this calculation, we want to know how much people would pay in monthly premium to reduce their deductible by $1,000.

The deductible coefficient (-0.0008) tells us that each additional $1 in deductible decreases utility by 0.0008 points.

Therefore, reducing the deductible by $1,000 increases utility by: 0.0008 × 1,000 = 0.8 points

To find the WTP, we ask: “How much premium increase would cause a utility decrease of 0.8 points?”

\[\text{WTP}_{\text{deductible}} = -\frac{\beta_{\text{deductible}} \times (-1000)}{\beta_{\text{premium}}} = -\frac{0.8000}{-0.0116} = \$68.97\]

Interpretation: Enrollees would be willing to pay an additional $68.97 per month to reduce their annual deductible by $1,000.

Summary of WTP Estimates:

Attribute WTP per Month
Large Network (vs. Small) $55.73
$1,000 Lower Deductible $68.97

Managerial Application:

These WTP values help managers make cost-benefit decisions:

  • Network expansion decision: If expanding the provider network costs the insurer $30 per member per month, and members are willing to pay $55.73 for it, the expansion is financially viable: the insurer can charge an additional $55.73 in premium while only incurring $30 in costs, creating a $25.73 per member margin.

  • Premium pricing for deductible tiers: Members are willing to pay $68.97 per month (or $828 annually) to reduce their deductible by $1,000. If the insurer’s expected claims savings from a $1,000 higher deductible is approximately $400 per year, they could price the premium difference at $68.97/month, capturing additional revenue while members still perceive value.

2.2.8 Market Share Simulation

One of the most powerful applications of conjoint analysis is predicting how the market would respond to different product configurations.

The Process for Rating-Based Conjoint:

  1. Define two (or more) competing options
  2. Calculate the predicted rating for each option using the regression model
  3. Convert ratings to market shares using proportional allocation

Proportional Allocation: If Plan A receives a predicted rating of 8 and Plan B receives 6, we allocate market share proportionally:

\[\text{Share}_A = \frac{8}{8+6} = 57\% \qquad \text{Share}_B = \frac{6}{8+6} = 43\%\]

Example Scenario:

Let’s compare two common plan designs:

  • Plan A (Premium Plan): $250/month premium, $500 annual deductible, Large network
  • Plan B (Budget Plan): $200/month premium, $1,500 annual deductible, Small network

Neither plan dominates the other: Plan A is better on deductible and network, while Plan B is better on premium.

# Define two competing health plans
plan_A <- data.frame(
  name = "Premium Plan",
  premium = 250,
  deductible = 500,
  network_large = 1,
  description = "Mid-price, Low deductible, Large network"
)

plan_B <- data.frame(
  name = "Budget Plan",
  premium = 200,
  deductible = 1500,
  network_large = 0,
  description = "Low-price, High deductible, Small network"
)

# Predict ratings for each plan
rating_A <- predict(model_rating_ins, plan_A)
rating_B <- predict(model_rating_ins, plan_B)

# Calculate market share using proportional allocation
total_rating <- rating_A + rating_B
share_A <- rating_A / total_rating
share_B <- rating_B / total_rating

Interpretation: Based on the predicted ratings, approximately 56% of enrollees would prefer the Premium Plan despite its higher cost, because the low deductible and large network compensate for the price difference. This helps managers:

  • Forecast enrollment in different plan designs
  • Understand competitive positioning
  • Optimize plan configurations

Note on Method: Proportional allocation is a simple and intuitive approach, but it lacks theoretical grounding. It assumes that if Plan A gets a rating of 8 and Plan B gets a rating of 6, then exactly 57% of people will choose Plan A (8 ÷ 14 = 0.57). However, in reality, people make discrete yes/no choices, not proportional splits—someone either picks Plan A or they don’t. The method assumes market shares directly mirror rating differences, which oversimplifies how people actually choose. For important strategic decisions, choice-based methods with multinomial logit formulas (shown later) are theoretically more sound and preferred in practice because they model the actual choice process.

2.3 Choice-Based Conjoint Analysis

2.3.1 Conceptual Overview

Choice-based conjoint (CBC), also known as a Discrete Choice Experiment (DCE), is considered the gold standard in conjoint analysis because it closely mimics real-world decision-making (Louviere, Hensher, & Swait, 2000).

Instead of rating profiles individually, respondents are shown choice sets containing two or more options and asked to pick their preferred one:

Choice Set 1:

Plan A Plan B
Premium $200 $300
Deductible $1,500 $500
Network Small Large

Which plan would you choose? ☐ Plan A ☐ Plan B

By forcing a choice, we ensure that respondents make explicit trade-offs. Someone who wants both low premium AND low deductible must decide which matters more.

2.3.2 Multinomial Logit Model

Choice-based conjoint uses multinomial logistic regression to analyze choice data. This method can handle choice sets with any number of alternatives (2, 3, 4, or more). We’ll use the mlogit package in R, which is the standard tool for discrete choice analysis.

Key Concept:

The model assumes people choose the alternative with the highest utility. The probability of choosing an alternative depends on its utility relative to all other options:

\[P(\text{Choose alternative } i) = \frac{e^{V_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{J} e^{V_j}}\]

where:

  • \(V_i = \beta_1 X_{i1} + \beta_2 X_{i2} + ... + \beta_k X_{ik}\) is the systematic utility
  • \(V_i\) is the systematic (predictable) component of utility based on observed attributes
  • There’s also a random component (\(\varepsilon_i\)) representing individual taste variation and unmeasured factors
  • “Systematic” means we can predict it from the attributes we measure
  • \(J\) is the total number of alternatives in the choice set
  • The coefficients (\(\beta_1, \beta_2, ..., \beta_k\)) are the part-worth utilities

Intuition: Alternatives with higher utility have exponentially higher choice probabilities. The model estimates the part-worth utilities (\(\beta\) coefficients) that best explain the observed choices.

2.3.3 Generating Choice Data

We create choice sets by randomly pairing profiles, then simulate choices based on assumed true utilities:

# Parameters for simulation
n_respondents <- 100
n_choice_sets <- 8  # Each respondent completes 8 choice tasks

choice_insurance <- data.frame()

for (resp in 1:n_respondents) {
  for (cs in 1:n_choice_sets) {
    # Randomly select two different profiles for this choice set
    profiles <- sample(1:8, 2)

    alt_A <- insurance_profiles[profiles[1], ]
    alt_B <- insurance_profiles[profiles[2], ]

    # Calculate utilities using assumed true values
    # True parameters imply:
    # - WTP for $1,000 deductible reduction ≈ $50-60/month
    # - WTP for large vs small network ≈ $40-50/month
    utility_A <- -0.012 * alt_A$premium -
                 0.0007 * alt_A$deductible +
                 0.6 * (alt_A$network == "Large") +
                 rnorm(1, 0, 1)  # Random component

    utility_B <- -0.012 * alt_B$premium -
                 0.0007 * alt_B$deductible +
                 0.6 * (alt_B$network == "Large") +
                 rnorm(1, 0, 1)

    # Choice: 1 if A chosen, 0 if B chosen
    choice_A <- as.integer(utility_A > utility_B)

    choice_insurance <- rbind(choice_insurance, data.frame(
      respondent_id = resp,
      choice_set = cs,
      # Alternative A attributes
      premium_A = alt_A$premium,
      deductible_A = alt_A$deductible,
      network_A = alt_A$network,
      # Alternative B attributes
      premium_B = alt_B$premium,
      deductible_B = alt_B$deductible,
      network_B = alt_B$network,
      # Choice outcome
      choice_A = choice_A
    ))
  }
}

2.3.4 Data Preparation for mlogit

The mlogit package requires data in “long format” where each row represents one alternative (not one choice set). We need to:

  1. Reshape from wide to long format
  2. Index the data so mlogit understands the nested structure

From Wide to Long Format:

Currently each row contains both alternatives side-by-side. We need to transform this so each alternative gets its own row:

Wide format (current):

respondent choice_set premium_A premium_B network_A network_B
1 1 200 300 Large Small

Long format (needed):

choice_set_id alternative premium network_large choice
1_1 A 200 1 1
1_1 B 300 0 0 
# Step 1: Create unique choice set IDs
choice_insurance <- choice_insurance |> 
  mutate(choice_set_id = paste0(respondent_id, "_", choice_set))

# Step 2: Reshape to long format
choice_long <- choice_insurance |> 
  pivot_longer(
    cols = c(premium_A, premium_B, deductible_A, deductible_B, network_A, network_B),
    names_to = c(".value", "alternative"),
    names_pattern = "(.*)_(.*)"
  ) %>%
  mutate(
    choice = case_when(
      alternative == "A" & choice_A == 1 ~ 1,
      alternative == "A" & choice_A == 0 ~ 0,
      alternative == "B" & choice_A == 1 ~ 0,
      alternative == "B" & choice_A == 0 ~ 1
    ),
    network_large = if_else(network == "Large", 1, 0)
  ) |>
  select(respondent_id, choice_set = choice_set_id, alternative,
         premium, deductible, network_large, choice) |>
  mutate(respondent_id = factor(respondent_id)) |>
  arrange(choice_set, alternative)

2.3.5 Indexing and Model Estimation

The dfidx() function prepares the data for mlogit by creating an indexed structure:

# Prepare data for mlogit
choice_for_mlogit <- choice_long %>%
  select(-respondent_id) %>% # exclude respondent_id variable
  as.data.frame()  # Convert to base data.frame

# Create indexed data format
choice_mlogit <- dfidx(choice_for_mlogit,
                       shape = "long",
                       choice = "choice",
                       idx = c("choice_set", "alternative"))

What dfidx() does: It adds special index attributes telling mlogit which rows belong to the same choice set. This is essential for correctly estimating choice probabilities.

Now we can estimate the model using the mlogit function in R:

# Fit multinomial logit model
model_mlogit_ins <- mlogit(choice ~ premium + deductible + network_large | 0,
                           data = choice_mlogit)

Interpretation: The estimated coefficients are the part-worth utilities, representing how much each attribute contributes to the systematic utility (\(V_i\)) of an alternative.

  • Premium (-0.0138): Each additional $1 increase in monthly premium decreases the utility of that plan option by 0.0138 points
  • Deductible (-0.0006): Each additional $1 increase in annual deductible decreases the utility of that plan option by 0.0006 points
  • Network_large (0.8017): Having a large network (vs. small) increases the utility of that plan option by 0.8017 points

How do utilities translate to choice probabilities?

The multinomial logit formula converts utilities into choice probabilities:

\[P(\text{Choose alternative } i) = \frac{e^{V_i}}{\sum_{j} e^{V_j}}\]

where \(V_i = \beta_{\text{premium}} \times \text{premium}_i + \beta_{\text{deductible}} \times \text{deductible}_i + \beta_{\text{network}} \times \text{network}_i\) = systematic utility of alternative \(i\)

Key insight: Higher utility → Higher probability of being chosen, but the relationship is nonlinear (exponential). A plan with utility of 2.0 is much more likely to be chosen than one with utility of 1.0, not just twice as likely.

2.3.6 Attribute Importance (Choice-Based)

The calculation is identical to rating-based conjoint:

  1. First, get the range of utilities for each attribute (highest - lowest)
  2. Then, calculate total range (sum all the ranges)
  3. Finally, get each range as a percentage of the total
# Use mlogit coefficients
coef_choice <- coef(model_mlogit_ins)

# Calculate utility ranges
range_premium_c <- abs(coef_choice["premium"] * (300 - 200))  # $100 difference
range_deductible_c <- abs(coef_choice["deductible"] * (1500 - 500))  # $1000 difference
range_network_c <- abs(coef_choice["network_large"] * 1)  # Binary (0: small vs. 1: large)

total_range_c <- range_premium_c + range_deductible_c + range_network_c

importance_choice_ins <- data.frame(
  attribute = c("Monthly Premium", "Annual Deductible", "Network Size"),
  utility_range = c(range_premium_c, range_deductible_c, range_network_c),
  importance_pct = c(range_premium_c, range_deductible_c, range_network_c) / total_range_c * 100
)

2.3.8 Market Share Simulation (Choice-Based)

For choice-based conjoint, we use the multinomial logit formula to predict market shares:

\[P(\text{Choose alternative } j) = \frac{e^{U_j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{J} e^{U_k}}\]

Let’s compare the same two plans:

Two Competing Health Insurance Plans
Plan Monthly Premium Annual Deductible Network Size
Plan A (Premium Plan) $250/month $500 Large
Plan B (Budget Plan) $200/month $1,500 Small 
# Calculate utilities for each plan
coef_mlogit <- coef(model_mlogit_ins)

utility_A_c <- coef_mlogit["premium"] * 250 +
               coef_mlogit["deductible"] * 500 +
               coef_mlogit["network_large"] * 1

utility_B_c <- coef_mlogit["premium"] * 200 +
               coef_mlogit["deductible"] * 1500 +
               coef_mlogit["network_large"] * 0

# Apply multinomial logit formula
share_A_c <- exp(utility_A_c) / (exp(utility_A_c) + exp(utility_B_c))
share_B_c <- exp(utility_B_c) / (exp(utility_A_c) + exp(utility_B_c))

market_share_choice <- data.frame(
  Plan = c("Premium Plan", "Budget Plan"),
  Premium = c("$250", "$200"),
  Deductible = c("$500", "$1500"),
  Network_Size = c("Large", "Small"),
  Total_Utility = round(c(utility_A_c, utility_B_c), 2),
  Market_Share = paste0(round(c(share_A_c, share_B_c) * 100, 1), "%")
)

3 Summary and Comparison

3.1 Method Comparison

Comparison of Conjoint Analysis Methods
Aspect Rating-Based Choice-Based
Data Collection Rate each profile (e.g., 1-10 scale) Choose preferred option from set
Analysis Method OLS Linear Regression Multinomial Logit (mlogit package)
Data Format Wide format (one row per profile) Long format (one row per alternative)
Intercept in Model Yes (baseline rating) No (only relative preferences matter)
Key Assumption Utility → Rating is linear Random utility maximization
Market Share Prediction Proportional allocation based on ratings Multinomial logit formula (theoretically grounded)
Realism Lower: no forced trade-offs Higher: mimics actual decisions
Implementation Complexity Simpler to design and analyze Requires data restructuring
When to Use Exploratory research, simple products When predicting actual choices matters

3.2 Key Takeaways

  1. Both methods yield similar part-worth utilities when properly designed, because they’re measuring the same underlying preferences

  2. Choice-based is generally preferred in academic research and practice because it better mimics real decision-making and forces explicit trade-offs

  3. Multinomial logit is the standard for choice-based conjoint: It handles any number of alternatives (2, 3, 4, or more) and is grounded in random utility theory

  4. Data structure matters: Choice-based conjoint requires “long format” data with proper indexing to identify which alternatives belong to each choice set

  5. Attribute importance and WTP use the same formulas across both methods—calculations rely on utility ranges and coefficients regardless of estimation method

  6. The key managerial insight: Conjoint analysis transforms subjective preferences into quantitative data that supports concrete decisions about pricing, feature prioritization, and competitive strategy
