Two-Way ANOVA

Introduction

Where We Left Off

Last week, we learned two ANOVA designs for comparing means across 3+ groups:

  • One-Way ANOVA: tests whether group means differ across 3+ independent groups
  • Repeated-Measures ANOVA: compares means when the same subjects are measured under multiple conditions


Both designs examined the effect of one factor at a time.

But in healthcare, outcomes are rarely influenced by a single factor.

The Limitation of One Factor

Let’s think about this case:

You invest $500,000 in a new nurse training program, and the overall competency score improves by 5 points. Sounds like a success, until you discover it boosted novice nurses’ scores by 15 points but decreased experienced nurses’ scores by 5 points.


  • A one-way ANOVA would only show the modest average improvement (training program = 5 points).
  • Two-way ANOVA would have revealed this pattern before hospital-wide rollout (training program x experience levels).

This Week’s Question

This week, we extend ANOVA to examine two factors simultaneously:


Does the effect of one factor depend on the level of another factor?


  • This is the concept of an interaction effect, the most important idea in this lecture.
  • In the previous example, the effect of training program depends on the level of nurse experience.

Today’s Learning Goals

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to:

  1. Distinguish between main effects and interaction effects
  2. Identify three types of interaction patterns from interaction plots
  3. Choose the correct two-way ANOVA design (between, within, mixed)
  4. Conduct two-way ANOVA in R using aov() and interpret the results
  5. Perform simple effects analysis using emmeans() when interactions are significant

What is Two-Way ANOVA?

Understanding “One-Way” vs. “Two-Way”

In ANOVA, the term “way” refers to the number of factors we examine:


One-Way ANOVA: Effect of one factor on an outcome

  • e.g., Does satisfaction differ across 4 departments?
  • Factor: Department (4 levels) → Outcome: Satisfaction score

Two-Way ANOVA: Effects of two factors simultaneously

  • e.g., Does pain reduction depend on treatment type AND severity?
  • Factor A: Treatment, Factor B: Severity → Outcome: Pain reduction

Why Study Two Factors Simultaneously?

Two-way ANOVA allows us to test three things simultaneously:

  1. Test the effect of Factor A (main effect of A)
  2. Test the effect of Factor B (main effect of B)
  3. Test whether the effect of A depends on B (interaction effect A × B)

Main Effects vs. Interaction Effects

Suppose we want to understand the effects of treatment and patient group on recovery score:

  • Main Effect: The effect of one factor on the outcome, averaged across all levels of the other factor.
    • Main effect of Treatment: Does the treatment affect recovery, regardless of patient group?
    • Main effect of Patient Group: Do patient groups differ in recovery, regardless of treatment?
  • Interaction Effect: The effect of one factor depends on the level of another factor.
    • The treatment improves recovery for severe patients but not for mild patients

When there’s a significant interaction, main effects can be misleading!

Overview of Two-Way ANOVA Designs

Design Factor A Factor B Example
Between-Subjects Different subjects Different subjects Treatment type × Patient severity
Within-Subjects Same subjects Same subjects Drug type × Time of day
Mixed Design Different subjects Same subjects Training program × Assessment time

Understanding Interaction Patterns

Pattern 1: Parallel Lines (No Interaction)


  • The new treatment improves outcomes by 15 points for both mild and severe patients

  • The treatment effect is consistent regardless of severity

  • Lines are parallel → No interaction

Pattern 2: Ordinal Interaction


  • New treatment helps both groups, but helps severe patients much more
    • Mild: +10 points
    • Severe: +25 points
  • Lines converge but don’t cross → Ordinal interaction

Pattern 3: Disordinal (Crossover) Interaction


  • New treatment helps severe patients (+30) but harms mild patients (-10)
  • The main effect might appear small or non-significant because effects cancel out!
  • Lines cross → Disordinal interaction

Why Interactions Matter in Healthcare

  1. Personalized medicine: Treatment effectiveness often varies by patient characteristics
  2. Resource allocation: Interventions may work better in some settings than others
  3. Safety: A treatment safe for one population may be harmful for another
  4. Policy decisions: Program effectiveness may depend on implementation context

1. Two-Way Between-Subjects ANOVA

What is it?

A two-way between-subjects ANOVA tests the effects of two factors where different participants are in each combination of factor levels.


Key Characteristics:

  • Two factors (each with 2+ levels)
  • Each participant is in only one cell of the design
  • Tests three effects: Main effect A, Main effect B, A × B interaction

When to Use It

Use when you have two categorical factors and each individual is assigned to only one combination:

  • Treatment types × patient demographics → pain scores, recovery time
    • e.g., Comparing opioid vs. non-opioid pain protocols across age groups (18–39, 40–64, 65+)
  • Interventions × facility types → length of stay, readmission rates
    • e.g., Evaluating a discharge planning program across urban, suburban, and rural hospitals
  • Training programs × experience levels → competency scores, error rates
    • e.g., Simulation-based vs. lecture-based training for novice vs. experienced nurses

Scenario: Treatment × Disease Severity

A clinical trial compares two treatments for chronic pain across three severity levels.

  • Treatments:
    • Standard Care: Physical therapy, OTC pain relievers, patient education (~$800/patient)
    • Novel Multimodal: CBT for pain, targeted exercise, non-opioid prescription (~$3,200/patient)
  • Severity Levels:
    • Mild (pain 1-3), Moderate (pain 4-6), Severe (pain 7-10)

Outcome: Pain reduction score (0-100, higher = better)

Research Questions

  1. Does the novel treatment reduce pain more than standard care? (main effect of treatment)
  1. Does pain reduction vary by baseline severity? (main effect of severity)
  1. Does the treatment effect depend on severity level? (interaction)


If the novel treatment only benefits severe patients, prescribing it for everyone would waste the extra $2,400 per patient.

The Formula

Two-way ANOVA partitions variance into four components:

\[SS_{total} = SS_A + SS_B + SS_{A \times B} + SS_{error}\]

Three F-statistics are calculated:

\[F_A = \frac{MS_A}{MS_{error}}, \quad F_B = \frac{MS_B}{MS_{error}}, \quad F_{A \times B} = \frac{MS_{A \times B}}{MS_{error}}\]

Degrees of freedom:

  • \(df_A = a - 1\)
  • \(df_B = b - 1\)
  • \(df_{A \times B} = (a-1)(b-1)\)
  • \(df_{error} = N - ab\)

The R Formula

The R formula for two-way between-subjects ANOVA uses * for main effects plus interaction:

aov(outcome ~ factorA * factorB, data = dataset)


The * is shorthand for treatment + severity + treatment:severity


Note: Unlike repeated-measures ANOVA from Week 6, there is no Error() term here because each participant appears in only one condition.

Hypotheses

  1. Main effect of treatment:
    • \(H_0\): \(\mu_{Standard} = \mu_{Novel}\)
    • \(H_A\): \(\mu_{Standard} \neq \mu_{Novel}\)
  1. Main effect of severity:
    • \(H_0\): \(\mu_{Mild} = \mu_{Moderate} = \mu_{Severe}\)
    • \(H_A\): At least one severity mean differs
  1. Interaction:
    • \(H_0\): The treatment effect is the same at every severity level
    • \(H_A\): The treatment effect depends on severity level

Demonstration: Running Two-Way ANOVA

# Two-way ANOVA
anova_pain <- aov(pain_reduction ~ treatment * severity, data = pain_data)
summary(anova_pain)

Interpreting the ANOVA Table

The output shows three F-tests:

  • treatment: Does pain reduction differ between treatments, averaging across severity?
  • severity: Does pain reduction differ across severity, averaging across treatments?
  • treatment:severity: Does the treatment difference vary across severity levels?


Recall the ANOVA table columns: Df (degrees of freedom), Sum Sq (variation), Mean Sq (\(SS/df\)), F value, Pr(>F) (p-value)

Results Interpretation

  • Main effect of treatment: Significant (p < 0.05). Pain reduction differs between treatments.
  • Main effect of severity: Significant (p < 0.05). Pain reduction differs across severity levels.
  • Interaction (treatment:severity): Significant (p < 0.05). The treatment effect is not uniform (it differs depending on severity).

When Interaction is Significant

The main effect tells us the average treatment difference collapsed across severity. But the interaction tells us this average is misleading.


The Novel treatment’s advantage:

  • Mild patients: ~2 points improvement
  • Severe patients: ~12 points improvement


An administrator who only looked at the main effect might conclude “the Novel treatment is better for everyone (7 vs. 13).” The interaction reveals the $2,400 extra cost may not be justified for mild cases.


When the interaction is significant, examine simple effects to understand the pattern.

Simple Effects Analysis

Simple effects examine the effect of one factor at each level of the other factor:

# Get estimated marginal means
emm <- emmeans(anova_pain, ~ treatment | severity)

The output shows the treatment difference (Standard minus Novel) at each severity level:

  • Mild: Modest improvement; not statistically significant
  • Moderate: Larger improvement; statistically significant
  • Severe: Largest improvement; statistically significant

2. Two-Way Within-Subjects ANOVA

What is it?

A two-way within-subjects ANOVA tests the effects of two factors where the same participants experience all combinations of both factors.


Key Characteristics:

  • Same participants measured under all conditions
  • Both factors are within-subjects (repeated measures)
  • More powerful than between-subjects designs
  • Requires fewer participants

When to Use It

Use when the same participants are measured under all combinations of two factors:

  • Same patients tested with different medications at different times of day → pain control scores
    • e.g., Each patient tries ibuprofen and acetaminophen in the morning and evening
  • Same staff evaluated on multiple tasks across multiple assessment periods → performance scores
    • e.g., Each nurse is assessed on medication administration and charting at months 1 and 6
  • Same clinics measured on different metrics in different seasons → satisfaction scores
    • e.g., Each clinic’s wait time and communication scores are tracked in summer vs. winter

Scenario: Medication Type × Time of Day

A clinical study examines whether medication effectiveness varies by time of administration. The same 20 patients receive each of three medications at two times.


Medications:

  • Med A: Standard pain reliever
  • Med B: Extended-release pain medication
  • Med C: Fast-acting pain medication

Time: Morning vs. Evening

Outcome: Effectiveness score (0-100, higher = better pain control)

Study Design: Each patient experiences all 6 conditions (3 medications × 2 times).

Understanding the Error Term

Since the same patients experience all conditions, we account for this using Error():

  • If the same person experiences all levels of a factor, that factor goes inside Error(id/...)


Here, each patient receives all 3 medications AND both time points, so both factors go inside Error():

aov(outcome ~ factorA * factorB + Error(subject_id / (factorA * factorB)),
    data = dataset)

Comparing All Formula Types

Design Formula Why?
Between-subjects aov(y ~ A * B) Different people in each cell
One-way repeated (Week 6) aov(y ~ A + Error(id/A)) Same people across levels of A
Two-way within (current) aov(y ~ A * B + Error(id/(A*B))) Same people across ALL combinations

Hypotheses

  1. Main effect of medication:
    • \(H_0\): \(\mu_{Med A} = \mu_{Med B} = \mu_{Med C}\)
    • \(H_A\): At least one medication mean differs
  1. Main effect of time:
    • \(H_0\): \(\mu_{Morning} = \mu_{Evening}\)
    • \(H_A\): \(\mu_{Morning} \neq \mu_{Evening}\)
  1. Interaction:
    • \(H_0\): The time effect is the same for all medications
    • \(H_A\): The time effect depends on medication

3. Mixed Design ANOVA

What is it?

A mixed design ANOVA combines both between-subjects and within-subjects factors. This is the most common design in healthcare research.


Key Characteristics:

  • Between-subjects factor: Different participants in each group (e.g., treatment groups)
  • Within-subjects factor: Same participants measured repeatedly (e.g., time points)
  • The interaction is usually the most important test: do the groups show different trajectories?

When to Use It

Use when different groups of participants are measured repeatedly:

  • Clinical trials: Treatment group × Time points → symptom severity
    • e.g., Drug vs. placebo groups measured at baseline, 4 weeks, and 12 weeks
  • Patient experience: Hospital unit × Survey domains → satisfaction scores
    • e.g., ICU vs. med-surg patients surveyed on communication, responsiveness, and discharge
  • Quality improvement: Intervention group × Measurement periods → readmission rates
    • e.g., Hospitals with vs. without a care coordinator tracked quarterly for one year

Scenario: Training Program × Assessment Time

A hospital compares three training programs for nursing competency. 60 nurses are randomly assigned to one program (between) and assessed at three time points (within).

Training Programs:

  • Traditional Lecture: Classroom instruction (~$200/nurse)
  • Simulation-Based: Hands-on practice with mannequins (~$800/nurse)
  • Blended Learning: Online theory + in-person practice (~$600/nurse)

Time Points: Pre, Post, 3 Months

Key Management Question: Do more expensive methods maintain their advantage over time?

Understanding the Error Term

Apply the same rule: Does the same person experience all levels of this factor?

  • Program (between-subjects): Each nurse is in only one program → NOT in Error()
  • Time (within-subjects): Each nurse is tested at all three time points → in Error()


aov(outcome ~ between_factor * within_factor + Error(subject_id / within_factor),
    data = dataset)

Complete Formula Comparison

Design Formula Error Term Contains
Between-subjects aov(y ~ A * B) Nothing (no repeated measures)
Within-subjects aov(y ~ A * B + Error(id/(A*B))) Both A and B
Mixed aov(y ~ A * B + Error(id/B)) Only within-subjects factor B

Hypotheses

  1. Main effect of program:
    • \(H_0\): \(\mu_{Traditional} = \mu_{Simulation} = \mu_{Blended}\)
    • \(H_A\): At least one program mean differs
  1. Main effect of time:
    • \(H_0\): \(\mu_{Pre} = \mu_{Post} = \mu_{3 Months}\)
    • \(H_A\): At least one time point mean differs
  1. Interaction:
    • \(H_0\): The time trajectory is the same for all programs
    • \(H_A\): The time trajectory differs across programs

  • time: Main effect of time (Significant): competency scores change over time
  • program:time: The interaction (Significant): programs show different trajectories!


  • Traditional starts equal but falls behind
  • Simulation and Blended maintain their gains

When Interaction is Significant

Without the interaction, an administrator might conclude all programs need refresher courses equally.

The interaction reveals:

  • Traditional: significant decay at 3 months
  • Simulation & Blended: retain most of their gains


When the interaction is significant, examine simple effects to understand the pattern.

Summary

Key Takeaways: Design Formulas

Design Between Factor Within Factor R Formula
Between A, B — aov(y ~ A * B)
Within — A, B aov(y ~ A*B + Error(id/(A*B)))
Mixed A B aov(y ~ A*B + Error(id/B))

Key Takeaways: Interaction Patterns

Pattern Lines Interpretation
No interaction Parallel Effect of A is same at all levels of B
Ordinal Converge/diverge Effect of A varies in magnitude but not direction
Disordinal Cross Effect of A reverses direction across levels of B

Choosing the Right Design

Ask yourself two questions about each factor:

  1. “Do different people experience different levels?” → Between-subjects factor
  2. “Does each person experience all levels?” → Within-subjects factor

Then match:

  • Both between → Two-Way Between-Subjects
  • Both within → Two-Way Within-Subjects
  • One of each → Mixed Design

Interpreting Results: Step-by-Step

  1. Check the interaction first
    • Significant → Focus on simple effects. Do not interpret main effects in isolation.
    • Not significant → Interpret main effects directly.
  2. For significant interactions, run simple effects
    • emmeans() with pairs() to compare at each level
  3. Connect to practical implications
    • What does this mean for clinical practice or resource allocation?
    • Which subgroups benefit most?
