Penalized Regression in Healthcare Analytics

Published

April 27, 2026

1 Introduction and Learning Objectives

1.1 Prediction vs. Explanation: A Critical Distinction

In previous courses, we learned to use regression to explain why things happen (e.g., understand which factors cause higher costs or worse outcomes). This module takes a different approach: we focus on prediction, how we can more accurately predict outcomes, rather than explanation.

What’s the difference?

  • Explanation: Understanding why certain patients cost more (e.g., “Does smoking cause higher costs?”)
  • Prediction: Forecasting how much a patient will cost (regardless of why)

When explaining, we care about individual variables and their effects. When predicting, we only care about accuracy (e.g., how well we can forecast outcomes for new patients). The combination of variables matters more than understanding each one.

1.2 Why Prediction Matters in Healthcare

Healthcare managers routinely need accurate forecasts:

  • Budget planning: How much will our patient panel cost next year?
  • Resource allocation: Which patients need care management outreach?
  • Readmission prevention: Who is at highest risk of coming back to the hospital?

Traditional regression often fails at prediction when we have many potential factors to consider, like age, diagnoses, medications, prior utilization, and more. With dozens or hundreds of variables, standard methods either break down mathematically or produce unreliable forecasts.

Penalized regression addresses this by penalizing overly complex models: it automatically reduces the influence of less important variables (sometimes to zero), keeping only what matters for prediction.

1.3 Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  1. Distinguish between prediction and explanation, and recognize when each approach is appropriate for healthcare management decisions

  2. Explain why standard regression struggles with prediction when you have many potential factors, and how penalized regression addresses this challenge

  3. Compare the three main types of penalized regression (Ridge, LASSO, and Elastic Net) and choose the right method for different situations

  4. Build penalized regression models in R to predict healthcare outcomes like costs and readmissions

  5. Interpret prediction results appropriately, understanding what the model tells you (future outcomes) and what it doesn’t tell you (causal relationships)

2 Conceptual Foundations

This section provides the theoretical foundation you need to understand penalized regression. We’ll start with a review of ordinary least squares regression, identify its limitations, and then see how adding a penalty term addresses these limitations.

2.1 Review: Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Regression

Before diving into penalized methods, let’s recall how standard linear regression works. This review will help us understand what we’re modifying and why.

2.1.1 The Basic Setup

In linear regression, we want to predict a continuous outcome variable \(Y\) (e.g., healthcare costs) using a set of predictor variables \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_p\) (e.g., age, diagnoses, prior utilization). We assume the relationship takes the form:

\[\hat{Y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p\]

Where:

  • \(\hat{Y}\) is our predicted value
  • \(\beta_0\) is the intercept (predicted value when all X’s are zero)
  • \(\beta_1, \beta_2, \ldots, \beta_p\) are coefficients that tell us how much \(Y\) changes for a one-unit change in each predictor

Note on terminology: Throughout this document, we use the terms “predictor,” “variable,” and “feature” interchangeably to refer to the X variables used to predict the outcome.

2.1.2 How OLS Finds the Best Coefficients

OLS finds the coefficient values that minimize the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE), the total squared difference between what we predicted and what actually happened:

\[\text{Minimize: } SSE = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i)^2\]

We square the prediction errors (so positive and negative errors don’t cancel out), add them up across all observations, and find the intercept and coefficients that make this sum as small as possible.

This approach works excellently when:

  • We have many more observations than predictors (\(n \gg p\))
  • Predictors are not highly correlated with each other
  • Our primary goal is to understand relationships and statistical inference (how each predictor affects the outcome)

However, many healthcare analytics applications violate these conditions, leading to several major problems.

2.2 Problems with Standard Regression in Healthcare

  1. Related variables create confusion

  • Healthcare factors naturally cluster together. For example, diabetes patients often have high blood pressure and obesity, patients with many doctor visits also have many prescriptions, etc.

  • When variables are related, standard regression can’t determine which one truly drives the outcome. This leads to unstable results: coefficients can become wildly inflated, have wrong signs, or change dramatically with small data changes.

  1. Too many variables, too few observations

  • Healthcare data often has hundreds of potential predictors (diagnosis codes, medications, lab tests) but limited patients. For example, a hospital might have 300 patients but 250 potential predictor variables (or even more predictors than patients)

  • Then, the model “memorizes” your specific patients rather than learning general patterns (overfitting). It predicts your current patients perfectly but fails miserably on new patients.

  1. Hard to identify what factors are really important

  • Standard regression uses every variable you give it, even irrelevant ones. You end up with all hundreds of variables in your model.

  • Healthcare organizations need interpretable, actionable models (clinicians can’t use hundreds of factors to make decisions).

2.3 The Solution: Penalized Regression

2.3.1 The Key Insight

Those problems above stem from too much flexibility (meaning OLS can set coefficients to any value to minimize training error). A potential solution is to constrain the coefficients, preventing them from becoming too large.

We do this by modifying the OLS objective function:

\[\text{Minimize: } SSE + \lambda \times \text{Penalty}\]

In plain language, find coefficients that balance prediction accuracy + penalty for model complexity

Where:

  • SSE = Sum of Squared Errors (how well the model fits the training data)
  • Penalty = A function of the coefficient sizes (how complex the model is)
  • λ (lambda) = A tuning parameter that controls how much we penalize complexity

2.3.2 How the Penalty Helps

Why does the penalty improve predictions? Without constraints, standard regression overfits; it “memorizes” the idiosyncrasies of your specific patients rather than learning general patterns. The penalty prevents this by keeping the model simple enough to work well on new patients. Think of it like studying concepts versus memorizing answers: concepts work on new problems, memorization doesn’t.

The penalty term creates a tradeoff:

  • Making SSE smaller (better fit) typically requires larger, more complex coefficients (lower bias, higher variance)
  • Making the penalty smaller requires smaller coefficients (higher bias, lower variance)
  • The optimal solution balances these competing goals: slightly worse fit on your current data in exchange for better predictions on new patients

How this addresses each problem:

  • Handles correlated variables: When variables are related (like diabetes, obesity, and blood pressure), the penalty prevents OLS’s typical instability; exaggerated coefficients, lost significance, or sign flips relative to single-variable models.

  • Prevents overfitting with many variables: The penalty stops the model from “memorizing” your specific patients. By shrinking coefficients, it forces the model to focus only on the strongest, most reliable patterns that will generalize to new patients.

  • Automatic variable selection (LASSO): The L1 penalty pushes weak predictors to exactly zero, eliminating them from the model entirely. You end up with a small set of important variables instead of hundreds.

2.3.3 The Role of Lambda (λ)

The tuning parameter λ controls how much we care about the penalty:

  • λ = 0: No penalty, model equals OLS
  • Small λ: Light penalty, close to OLS
  • Large λ: Heavy penalty, coefficients shrunk toward zero
  • λ → ∞: All coefficients forced to zero (except intercept)

We need to choose λ carefully (too small and we don’t solve our problems; too large and we throw away useful information). We’ll discuss how to select λ in practice (using cross-validation) after introducing the three penalized regression methods.

3 Penalized Regression Models

3.1 Ridge Regression: The L2 Penalty

Now let’s examine the first specific type of penalized regression: Ridge regression. This method addresses multicollinearity and overfitting by shrinking all coefficients toward zero.

3.1.1 Mathematical Formulation

Ridge regression adds a penalty based on the squared values of coefficients:

\[\text{Minimize: } \sum_{i=1}^{n} (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2\]

The penalty term \(\sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2\) is called the L2 penalty or squared penalty because we’re summing squared coefficients.

What this formula says:

“Find the coefficients that make predictions accurate (small SSE) while also keeping coefficients small (small sum of squared betas). The parameter λ controls how much we care about keeping coefficients small.”

3.1.2 How Ridge Regression Works

Ridge regression works by gently discouraging large coefficients. Instead of allowing the model to rely too heavily on any single predictor, it nudges all coefficients toward smaller values. This has several important consequences.

  1. Ridge regression shrinks all coefficients toward zero, but does not remove variables.

As the penalty becomes stronger, coefficient values get smaller and smaller. However, they do not drop all the way to zero. This means Ridge regression keeps all predictors in the model, but limits how strongly each one can influence the outcome.

  1. Larger coefficients are shrunk more than smaller ones.

Ridge regression is especially uncomfortable with very large coefficients. If one predictor has a much larger effect than others, the penalty pushes it down more strongly. Smaller coefficients are affected less. This helps prevent the model from overreacting to noise or peculiarities in the data.

  1. Ridge regression handles correlated predictors smoothly.

When predictors are highly correlated, ordinary regression often struggles to decide which one deserves credit, leading to unstable or counterintuitive estimates. Ridge regression avoids this problem by sharing the effect across correlated variables. Instead of picking one variable and inflating its coefficient, it assigns moderate coefficients to both, producing more sensible and reliable results.

3.1.3 Visual Understanding: The Constraint Region

One of the best ways to understand Ridge regression is geometrically. Imagine we have just two predictors, so we’re looking for β₁ and β₂.

Understanding the figure:

  • Red ellipses: Contour lines of SSE. The center is where SSE is minimized (the OLS solution). Moving away from the center increases SSE.

  • Blue shaded region: The constraint region; all combinations of β₁ and β₂ that satisfy the penalty constraint. For Ridge, this is a circle because we’re constraining \(\beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2 \leq c\) (equation of a circle).

  • The solution: We want to be as close to the OLS center as possible (small SSE) while staying within the constraint region (small penalty). The Ridge solution is where the (smallest) ellipse touches the circle.

Key insight: Because the constraint region is a circle, the solution rarely lands exactly on an axis. This means Ridge keeps all variables with non-zero coefficients.

3.1.4 Advantages and Disadvantages of Ridge Regression

Ridge’s defining feature (shrinking but never removing predictors) is both its strength and its limitation. The same property that stabilizes correlated variables and protects against overfitting also makes the model hard to interpret and unable to discard noise.

Advantages Disadvantages
Stabilizes correlated predictors. Limits the coefficient inflation, lost significance, and sign flips that OLS produces under multicollinearity, spreading influence across related variables. Limited interpretability. Effects are split across many small coefficients, making it hard to identify which predictors truly matter. Better suited for prediction than explanation.
Better prediction on new data. The penalty prevents overfitting, often outperforming OLS on unseen patients. Retains noisy predictors. Irrelevant variables are weakened but never removed; many noise variables together can still degrade performance.
Keeps all relevant information. When most predictors plausibly matter at least a little, Ridge retains them all rather than discarding any. Hard to simplify or deploy. Keeping every predictor makes the model unwieldy for clinical or operational use, where simpler models are often required.

3.1.5 When to Use Ridge Regression

Ridge regression is best when:

  • Predictors are strongly correlated
  • Prediction is the main goal (interpretation is less important)
  • Most variables are expected to carry some information (so, you don’t need automatic variable selection)

3.2 LASSO Regression: The L1 Penalty

LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) is the other major penalized regression method. Its key advantage over Ridge is automatic feature selection: LASSO can eliminate variables entirely by setting their coefficients exactly to zero.

3.2.1 Mathematical Formulation

LASSO adds a penalty based on the absolute values of coefficients:

\[\text{Minimize: } \sum_{i=1}^{n} (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_j|\]

The penalty term \(\sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_j|\) is called the L1 penalty (because it is the L1 norm of the coefficient vector — the sum of absolute values).

The critical difference from Ridge is that LASSO uses absolute values instead of squared values, which changes the geometry in a way that allows coefficients to become exactly zero.

3.2.2 How LASSO Achieves Feature Selection

LASSO’s key feature selection property comes from its constraint shape (see the figure in the Ridge section above, which shows both the diamond-shaped LASSO constraint and the circular Ridge constraint).

The LASSO constraint has sharp corners along the axes. When the model searches for the best fit, it often lands on one of these corners, where one or more coefficients are exactly zero.

In contrast, Ridge regression uses a smooth, round constraint. This makes it unlikely for coefficients to be driven all the way to zero, so all predictors remain in the model.

3.2.3 Advantages and Disadvantages of LASSO Regression

LASSO’s defining feature (setting weak coefficients exactly to zero) is both its strength and its limitation. The same property that produces sparse, interpretable models also forces arbitrary choices among correlated predictors and discards information from variables that carry partial signal.

Advantages Disadvantages
Automatic feature selection. Identifies a small set of important predictors and removes the rest, controlling overfitting and producing a sparse model that generalizes better. Unstable with correlated predictors. When predictors are highly correlated, LASSO arbitrarily picks one and drops the others; which variable is chosen can vary across samples, making interpretation fragile.
Interpretability. With fewer active predictors, the model’s logic is easier to understand and explain in practical terms. May discard useful information. Setting coefficients exactly to zero can eliminate predictors that still carry some signal. In purely predictive tasks, Ridge may perform slightly better.
Effective in high-dimensional settings. Works even when the number of predictors exceeds the number of observations (p > n), common with diagnostic codes, procedures, or lab measures. Selection limited in very small samples. When the sample size is small, LASSO can only select a limited number of predictors, potentially missing relevant factors.

3.2.4 When to Use LASSO Regression

LASSO is best when:

  • Identifying key predictors is a primary goal
  • Simplicity and interpretability matters
  • You have many potential predictors and need feature selection
  • Predictors are not highly correlated

3.3 Elastic Net

Elastic Net combines the strengths of both Ridge and LASSO by using both penalties together. It’s often the best choice in practice because it handles correlated variables better than LASSO while still performing feature selection.

3.3.1 Mathematical Formulation

Elastic Net minimizes:

\[\text{Minimize: } \sum_{i=1}^{n} (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i)^2 + \lambda \left[ \alpha \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_j| + (1-\alpha) \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2 \right]\]

Where:

  • α (alpha) controls the mix of L1 and L2 penalties
  • α = 1: Pure LASSO
  • α = 0: Pure Ridge
  • 0 < α < 1: Elastic Net blend

3.3.2 How Elastic Net Handles Correlated Variables

When predictors are strongly correlated, LASSO tends to select one and discard the rest, even if they all carry meaningful information. Ridge takes the opposite approach, keeping everything and shrinking coefficients together. Elastic Net sits between these two.

The key idea is the grouping effect. Elastic Net tends to keep or remove groups of related variables together, rather than making arbitrary choices among them. The Ridge component encourages correlated predictors (or groups) to stay together, while the LASSO component removes predictors (or groups) that contribute little to prediction.

For example, imagine you include several variables that capture a patient’s overall health status in different ways (e.g., past diagnoses, prior hospital use, overall medical complexity, etc.) along with many unrelated predictors.

  • LASSO might keep one of these health measures and drop others
  • Ridge would keep all measures, including many irrelevant ones
  • Elastic Net would likely keep all of the health measures together, while removing unrelated noise variables

3.3.3 Tuning Elastic Net

Elastic Net balances two competing goals: simplicity and stability. It uses two parameters to control this balance.

  1. λ (lambda): How strongly coefficients are penalized overall
  2. α (alpha): How much the model behaves like LASSO vs. Ridge

In practice, the model tries different balances and selects the one that performs best on new data. Rather than committing to LASSO or Ridge upfront, Elastic Net lets the data decide how much feature selection and how much stabilization are needed.

3.3.4 Advantages and Disadvantages of Elastic Net

Elastic Net’s defining feature (combining L1 and L2 penalties through the α parameter) is both its strength and its limitation. The same flexibility that lets it perform feature selection while handling correlated predictors also adds tuning complexity and may be unnecessary when a single penalty would suffice.

Advantages Disadvantages
Combines feature selection and stability. Removes unimportant predictors (LASSO-like) while keeping correlated, meaningful ones together (Ridge-like), and produces more consistent selections across samples than pure LASSO. More tuning required. Combining both penalties introduces an extra parameter (α) on top of λ, increasing computational cost and the risk of overfitting the tuning process itself.
Highly adaptable. Adjusting α lets the model be tailored to different data structures and research goals, from pure Ridge (α = 0) to pure LASSO (α = 1) and anywhere in between. Not always necessary. If predictors are largely independent, LASSO alone may suffice. If feature selection is unnecessary, Ridge alone may be simpler. The added flexibility is wasted when the data doesn’t need it.
Effective in very high dimensions. Unlike LASSO (which selects at most n predictors), Elastic Net can retain more predictors when the number of variables far exceeds the number of observations (p >> n). -

3.3.5 When to Use Elastic Net

Elastic Net is often a strong default choice, especially when:

  • Predictors are correlated and feature selection is desired
  • You are not sure whether Ridge or LASSO is more appropriate
  • The data are high-dimensional
  • Stability in selected predictors matters

Elastic Net is often the best starting choice because it combines Ridge’s stability with LASSO’s feature selection. If you’re unsure which method to use, start with Elastic Net and let cross-validation determine the optimal balance between Ridge and LASSO.

3.5 Choosing Lambda: Cross-Validation

All three methods share the same practical question: how do we pick the right λ?

We can’t just pick the λ that works best on our current patients. That would be like a student memorizing practice test answers — they ace the practice but fail on new questions on the real exam. We need to know which λ will work best on new patients we haven’t seen yet.

The solution: Cross-validation!

Think of cross-validation as creating multiple “practice tests”:

  1. Divide your data into 10 groups (called “folds”; the number of groups can vary)
  2. For each λ value you want to try:
    • Train the model on 9 groups, test on the 10th group
    • Repeat 10 times, each time holding out a different group
    • Average the prediction errors across all 10 tests
  3. Pick the λ with the lowest average error

Why this works: Every patient gets used for both training AND testing (just not at the same time). This efficiently uses all your data to estimate how well each λ will perform on new patients.

In practice: The software (like R’s glmnet package) does this automatically via the cv.glmnet function. You’ll see which λ performs best in the hands-on section that follows.

Problem: Imagine you work for a health insurance company. Every year, your company needs to predict how much each patient will cost in healthcare spending so you can:

  • Set appropriate insurance premiums
  • Budget for the coming year
  • Identify high-cost patients who might benefit from care management programs (like helping diabetics control their blood sugar to avoid expensive complications)

Your Task: Build a model that predicts next year’s total healthcare spending for each patient based on information you currently have about them.

Available Data: You have data on 80 patients from a small specialty clinic, including the following clinically meaningful predictors:

The Challenge: With only 80 patients but 29 candidate predictors (many incidental, some correlated), standard OLS regression is likely to overfit. We’ll use penalized regression to build a model that predicts well on new patients.

4.5 Ridge Regression

Now let’s fit a Ridge regression model using the L2 penalty.

4.5.1 Fitting the Ridge Model

Recall that Ridge adds an L2 penalty to OLS: \(\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 + \lambda \sum \beta_j^2\). We implement this in R using the glmnet() function from the glmnet package, where the alpha argument selects which penalty to apply:

  • alpha = 0 → pure Ridge (L2 only)
  • alpha = 1 → pure LASSO (L1 only)
  • 0 < alpha < 1 → Elastic Net (blend of L1 and L2)

Note that glmnet() requires the predictors as a matrix (x_train) and the outcome as a vector (y_train).

# Fit Ridge regression (alpha = 0)
ridge_model <- glmnet(
  x = x_train,
  y = y_train,
  alpha = 0
)

The glmnet package automatically tests a sequence of lambda values to explore different levels of penalty strength.

4.5.2 Visualizing the Coefficient Paths

Before picking a single λ, it helps to see how the coefficients behave across the full range of penalty values that glmnet() tested. A coefficient path plot shows each predictor as a line, traced across different λ values, making it easy to visualize how the penalty affects each variable’s coefficient.

# Plot coefficient paths
plot(ridge_model, xvar = "lambda", label = TRUE)
title("Ridge Regression: Coefficient Paths", line = 3)

Interpretation of the plot:

  • X-axis: -Log(λ) (negative log lambda).
    • Left = large penalty (heavy shrinkage)
    • Right = small penalty (close to OLS)
  • Y-axis: Coefficient values
  • Each line: Each predictor variable
  • Key observation: As we move left on the plot (larger λ, stronger penalty), all coefficients shrink toward zero, but none reach exactly zero

4.5.3 Cross-Validation to Find Optimal Lambda

Now let’s use cross-validation to find the optimal λ. The cv.glmnet() function automates the entire process: it tests a sequence of λ values, evaluates each one with k-fold cross-validation, and reports which performs best.

The nfolds = 10 argument tells the function to use 10-fold cross-validation; split the training data into 10 groups, fit the model 10 times (each time holding one fold out as a mini test set), and average the prediction errors across folds. Ten folds is the most common choice in practice: large enough for stable estimates, small enough to stay computationally fast.

# Perform 10-fold cross-validation
ridge_cv <- cv.glmnet(
  x = x_train,
  y = y_train,
  alpha = 0,
  nfolds = 10 # how many folds will be used
)

# Plot CV results
plot(ridge_cv)
title("Ridge Regression: Cross-Validation Results", line = 3)

The cross-validation plot shows prediction error (vertical axis) for different lambda values (horizontal axis). The two vertical dashed lines mark the two lambda options that cross-validation identifies.

Understanding the two lambda choices:

Cross-validation provides two options:

  • lambda.min: The λ with the absolute lowest prediction error (best accuracy)
  • lambda.1se: Uses the “1 standard error rule”: selects the largest λ (simplest model) whose prediction error is within 1 standard error of the minimum. This provides a simpler, more robust model with negligible loss in accuracy.

4.5.5 Ridge Test Set Performance

Now we evaluate the Ridge model on the test set (the 30% of data we held out at the start) to see how well it predicts for patients the model has never seen.

The predict() function generates Ridge predictions for the test data, with two key arguments:

  • s = "lambda.1se" specifies which λ to use (the simpler model selected by cross-validation)
  • newx = x_test is the test predictor matrix (must be a matrix, just like x_train)

Ridge’s test RMSE is $3,959 versus OLS’s $5,730, an improvement of 30.91%, or about $1,771 closer per patient on average. In other words, Ridge produces noticeably better predictions for new patients than OLS.

Why does Ridge help here? This dataset has the exact conditions under which penalization pays off:

  • Small n relative to p (only 80 patients for 29 predictors, leaving just 56 training observations to estimate 29 coefficients): OLS estimates are unstable and overfit to the training data. Ridge’s shrinkage adds a small amount of bias but dramatically reduces variance.
  • Many irrelevant predictors: 12 of the 29 variables are administrative fields with no real effect on cost, but OLS still assigns them non-zero coefficients that chase noise in the training data. Ridge shrinks those coefficients toward zero (though, as we saw above, it never sets them exactly to zero).
  • Some correlation among chronic conditions (e.g., diabetes ↔︎ hypertension ↔︎ heart disease): Ridge handles this gracefully by spreading effects across correlated variables, rather than producing the unstable, sign-flipping coefficients OLS can yield under multicollinearity.

Notice, however, that Ridge keeps all 29 variables in the model; it just shrinks each coefficient toward zero. This makes the model harder to interpret because you can’t easily identify which predictors actually matter. Next, we’ll see whether LASSO can do even better by eliminating the irrelevant predictors entirely.

4.6 LASSO Regression

Now let’s fit a LASSO model using the L1 penalty for automatic feature selection.

4.6.1 Fitting the LASSO Model

Recall that LASSO uses an L1 penalty instead of L2: \(\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 + \lambda \sum |\beta_j|\). Because the penalty is based on absolute values rather than squares, LASSO can drive some coefficients exactly to zero, effectively removing those variables from the model. This is what makes LASSO uniquely useful for automatic feature selection.

The R code looks almost identical to Ridge. We use the same glmnet() function with the same x_train matrix and y_train vector. The only change is alpha = 1 to switch from L2 to L1 penalty.

# Fit LASSO regression (alpha = 1)
lasso_model <- glmnet(
  x = x_train,
  y = y_train,
  alpha = 1
)

4.6.2 Visualizing LASSO Coefficient Paths

Unlike Ridge, the LASSO paths hit zero. As we move left on the plot (larger λ, stronger penalty), variables are progressively eliminated. This is feature selection.

4.7 Elastic Net Regression

Finally, let’s fit an Elastic Net model that combines Ridge and LASSO penalties.

4.7.1 Tuning Alpha and Lambda

For the alpha parameter (weights of L1 and L2 regularization), we will use a grid search method (starting from 0, we will increase alpha by 0.1 every time).

# Define grid of alpha values
alpha_grid <- seq(0, 1, by = 0.1)

# Store results
elastic_results <- data.frame(
  alpha = alpha_grid,
  lambda_1se = NA,
  mse_1se = NA,
  n_features = NA
)

# Use consistent CV folds (10)
fold_id <- sample(1:10, size = length(y_train), replace = TRUE) 

# Test each alpha value
for (i in seq_along(alpha_grid)) {
  cv_fit <- cv.glmnet(
    x = x_train,
    y = y_train,
    alpha = alpha_grid[i],
    foldid = fold_id # instead of nfolds, we use foldid approach to have the same data in each fold 
  )

  elastic_results$lambda_1se[i] <- cv_fit$lambda.1se
  
  # Get MSE at lambda.1se
  idx_1se <- which(cv_fit$lambda == cv_fit$lambda.1se)
  elastic_results$mse_1se[i] <- cv_fit$cvm[idx_1se]

  coef_temp <- coef(cv_fit, s = "lambda.1se")
  elastic_results$n_features[i] <- sum(coef_temp != 0) - 1
}

5 Practice: Predicting Hospital Length of Stay

5.1 Background and Context

The Business Problem:

You work for the operations team at Metropolitan General Hospital, a 400-bed facility. One of your biggest challenges is managing patient flow, and knowing how long patients will stay helps you:

  • Plan bed capacity: Ensure enough beds are available for incoming patients
  • Schedule staffing: Assign the right number of nurses and support staff each day
  • Coordinate discharges: Start discharge planning early for patients expected to stay longer
  • Reduce bottlenecks: Prevent emergency department overcrowding when hospital beds are full

When a patient is admitted, you need to predict their length of stay (how many days they’ll be in the hospital). The problem is complex because many factors influence how long a patient stays:

  • Some factors are obvious (older patients tend to stay longer)
  • Many factors are correlated (patients admitted through the emergency department often have more complications)
  • You have dozens of potential predictors but limited historical data

This is where penalized regression helps: it can handle many correlated predictors and identify which factors truly matter for predicting length of stay.

6 Summary and Key Takeaways

6.1 Three Methods at a Glance

Method Penalty Feature selection Tuning Best for
Ridge L2 (squared) No — keeps all 1 (λ) Correlated predictors, all relevant
LASSO L1 (absolute) Yes — zeros out 1 (λ) Sparse, interpretable models
Elastic Net L1 + L2 Yes 2 (λ + α) Feature selection with correlated predictors (good default)

6.2 Key Takeaways

  1. Penalized regression trades a small amount of bias for a large reduction in variance: often outperforming OLS when n is small relative to p, predictors are correlated, or many predictors carry no signal.

  2. Two parameters control the penalty: α selects the type (0 = Ridge, 1 = LASSO, in between = Elastic Net); λ selects the strength. Both must be tuned with cross-validation.

  3. Use lambda.1se (the largest λ within 1 standard error of the CV minimum) for simpler, more robust models. Use lambda.min only when prediction accuracy is the only concern.

  4. LASSO performs automatic feature selection by setting some coefficients exactly to zero. Ridge shrinks but keeps all variables; Elastic Net does both.

  5. Penalized regression is for prediction, not causal inference. Coefficients are biased toward zero and lack standard errors; selected variables are associated with, not necessarily causes of, the outcome. Validate selected variables clinically before acting on them.

  6. Always evaluate final performance on a held-out test set, separate from the data used for cross-validation. CV is for tuning; the test set is for honest reporting.
